# 05 · Ed McBain — The 87th Precinct

> **Card** · 1956–2005 · 55 novels · ensemble police procedural · 3rd person rotating / past · Isola
> (fictional New York)
> **Read first:** *Cop Hater* (1956)
> **Popularity:** 100+ million copies; 49 years of continuous publication; the acknowledged template for
> the ensemble procedural and for *Hill Street Blues*.
> **Voice card:** sentence 4 · variance 5 · figuration 2 · interiority 4 · dialogue 8 · description 3 ·
> violence 5 · irony 5 · fatalism 4 · procedure 8 · sex 3 · distance 7

## What the series solved

McBain solved the **problem of the squad**. Earlier crime fiction gave us one detective with one case;
McBain gave us a shift, a building, and three or four cases running at once, with the personal lives of
the detectives threaded through the procedural work. The result is a form that can carry comedy, tedium,
bureaucracy, and sudden death without any of them cancelling the others.

His other great discovery was **tone management**. The books are dry, fast, and often funny, but the comedy
comes from the gap between institutional language and human mess — a detective with a head cold, a
paperwork error that costs a day, a witness whose testimony is mostly about her neighbour's dog. McBain's
own account of how he made cops sympathetic is the best craft note in the genre: *give them head colds,
first names, and homey dialogue.*

## Syntax and prose mechanics

- **Sentence.** Short, functional, journalistic. Subject-verb-object dominates. Occasional fragments for
  pace ("No answer.").
- **Paragraph.** Scene-based and short. A paragraph establishes place, delivers dialogue, and exits.
  Description is rationed to one or two details per scene.
- **Dialogue.** The engine. Lots of it, often untagged, with speakers identified by idiom and attitude
  rather than by "he said." Overlaps, interruptions, and non-answers are common.
- **Description.** Minimal and utilitarian. Weather matters because it affects the work; a room matters
  because of what can be seen from the door.
- **Figuration.** Very low. When a metaphor appears it is usually comic or grimly practical.
- **Punctuation.** Clean and conventional. Exclamation marks are reserved for the comic register.
- **Exposition.** Embedded in procedure: reports, lab results, phone calls, a detective explaining a step
  to a colleague who already half knows it.

## Character psychology

**Carella** is the series' centre of gravity: competent, stubborn, decent, and tired. His marriage to Teddy
— deaf, and rendered by McBain with a period's limitations as well as tenderness — gives the series its
domestic anchor. The other detectives are defined by **running gag plus private cost**: Meyer Meyer's
name and his father's joke; Cotton Hawes's handsomeness and his guilt; Bert Kling's youth and his losses;
Monaghan and Monroe's double act.

McBain's psychology is **behavioural and situational**. He does not enter his detectives' heads deeply; he
shows them in rooms, making choices under pressure and then going home. The effect is a powerful sense of
institutional personality: people become who their job requires them to be, and then occasionally surprise
themselves.

**Antagonists** are often ordinary and opportunistic rather than grand. The series' real interest is in
the **contingency** of crime: a break, a coincidence, a neighbour who happened to look out a window.

## Signature devices

1. **Multi-case braid.** Cut between two or three investigations so that each comments on the others.
2. **Head colds and first names.** Humanise the institution with small physical indignities.
3. **Untagged dialogue.** Let idiom identify the speaker.
4. **The procedural paper trail.** Show the form, the file, the lab slip; let the reader feel the friction.
5. **The comic chorus.** Recurring minor characters (Monaghan and Monroe) provide tonal relief without
   lowering the stakes.

## What to steal / what to avoid

**Steal:** the ensemble braid; humour as a realistic response to horror; procedure as texture rather than
lecture.

**Avoid:** the case that solves itself by coincidence without earning the coincidence; the running gag that
flattens a character into a type; period attitudes presented without critique (the series' handling of
Teddy's deafness and of women generally has dated).

## Diagnostic passage (original, in the voice)

> By the time Carella got there the ambulance had been and gone and the alley smelled of the rain that had
> fallen at four and the garbage that had been there since Thursday. The dead man lay on his back with one
> arm out, as if he had been trying to flag down a cab. Meyer came around the corner with two cups of
> coffee and handed him one.
>
> "Who is he?"
>
> "No wallet. No keys. A laundry ticket from a place on Hennessy that closed in March."
>
> "So he's been dead since March."
>
> "That's a joke, Steve."
>
> "I know it's a joke."
>
> The lab boys were still working the far end of the alley. A woman in a housecoat stood on the back steps
> of the building and watched them with the patient interest of someone who had seen worse on television.

**Annotation**

- Sentences are short and declarative; the one long sentence (the woman on the steps) carries the social
  observation and the joke.
- Dialogue is untagged after the first exchange. The voices are distinguished by function: Meyer reports,
  Carella deadpans.
- The humour is not a break from death; it is what people say while standing next to it.
- Procedure is present but not explained. The reader infers the chain of custody from the objects.

## Exercise

Write a 600-word squad-room chapter with three detectives and two unrelated cases. Constraints: no
detective solves anything; at least one scene is interrupted by a phone call; one character has a minor
physical ailment; end on a mundane administrative detail.
