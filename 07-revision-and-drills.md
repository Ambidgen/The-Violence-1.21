# 07 · Revision and Drills

Craft is not knowledge; it is a set of habits that survive contact with a draft. This chapter is the
workbook: thirty drills, seven revision passes, and a diagnostic interview you can run on your own book.

---

## 1. How to use the drills

- Do one drill per session, not five.
- Write by hand if you are stuck; it slows the sentence down to the speed of thought.
- Keep every drill in a single file. Date them. Re-read them in a month; the improvement will be obvious in
  the places you cannot see now.
- Do not revise a drill while writing it. Finish, then revise once.

---

## 2. Thirty drills

### Group A — The sentence (1–5)

**1. The Hammett cut.** Take 500 words you have written. Remove every adjective and adverb. Restore only
the ones that carry information. Read aloud.

**2. The Chandler swing.** Write a paragraph of exactly five sentences: long, long, short, long, very short.
The last sentence must be a judgment disguised as a joke.

**3. The Ellroy compression.** Write 200 words about a crime scene. Then cut it to 120 without removing a
single event. Then cut it to 80.

**4. The Burke tide.** Write a 60-word sentence about a landscape that contains three sensory details and
one threat. Follow it with a four-word sentence.

**5. The Child measure.** Write a scene in which a character enters a room and assesses it. No sentence over
12 words; no metaphor; at least five numerical or spatial observations.

### Group B — Dialogue (6–10)

**6. The transaction.** Write a 300-word conversation in which two people discuss lunch and negotiate a
bribe. Neither names the bribe.

**7. The idiolect.** Write the same piece of information ("the body was found at six") in the voices of
five different characters: a cop, a journalist, a child, a guilty spouse, a pathologist.

**8. The unanswered question.** Write a 200-word interview in which the detective asks three questions and
receives one answer.

**9. The tag purge.** Take a dialogue scene and remove every tag and action beat. Add back only what is
necessary for clarity. Count how few you need.

**10. The Leonard skip.** Write a scene that begins after the decision and ends before the explanation.

### Group C — Description (11–15)

**11. One detail.** Describe five characters using one object each. No adjectives of appearance.

**12. The power map.** Describe a building so that the reader understands who owns it, who works in it, and
who is excluded from it. Do not name a class.

**13. The smell.** Write 150 words in which the dominant sense is smell. No visual description until the
last sentence.

**14. The weather that acts.** Write a scene in which weather changes what a character can do. No weather
opening.

**15. The uncanny ordinary.** Write a description of a domestic object that becomes threatening without
using supernatural language.

### Group D — Point of view and psychology (16–20)

**16. The same room, three heads.** Describe a room from the POV of a cop, a victim's mother, and the
killer. Each version must notice different things and use different vocabulary.

**17. The free indirect.** Write 300 words in third person limited, then rewrite it in free indirect style.
Remove every "she thought" and "he wondered."

**18. The trauma tell.** Write a scene in which a character is triggered. Do not name the trauma. Use one
sensory trigger, one behaviour, one cost.

**19. The addiction ritual.** Write a scene in which a character decides not to drink. Make the decision
physical and specific. No monologue about willpower.

**20. The villain's justification.** Write 400 words from the POV of an antagonist explaining why they are
right. Do not make them a monster; make them coherent.

### Group E — Structure (21–25)

**21. The scene spine.** Write a scene with a want, an obstacle, a turn, a cost, and an exit. Label the
beats in the margin.

**22. The false solution.** Outline a case with three suspects. Make the most sympathetic one the false
solution and the least visible one the culprit. Ensure the reader has met both.

**23. The chapter cut.** Write a chapter that ends on a discovery, then a second version that ends on a cut.
Compare which creates more forward pressure.

**24. The dossier.** Build a 1,000-word investigation from four documents: a report, a letter, a receipt, a
transcript. No narrator commentary.

**25. The ending image.** Write the first and last paragraphs of a novel. The last image must answer the
first without repeating it.

### Group F — Ethics and pressure (26–30)

**26. The victim's life.** Write 300 words about a murder victim before the crime. No mention of the
detective.

**27. The aftermath.** Write the scene after a violent confrontation: the hospital, the paperwork, the
shower, the phone call to a family member. No action.

**28. The sex scene.** Write a sexual encounter between two adults in which what matters is a power shift.
No explicit anatomy; the scene must change the relationship.

**29. The moral injury.** Write a scene in which a character follows an order they believe is wrong. Show
the moment of compliance and the moment after.

**30. The silence.** Write a 300-word scene in which the most important thing is never said. No interiority
explaining it.

## 3. Seven revision passes

Do them in order. Do not combine.

### Pass 1 — Structure

- Outline the book from the draft, not from your memory. Mark the case spine. Where does the question
  change? Where does the detective lose something? Is the false solution plausible?
- Cut any scene that does not change the question, the position, or the reader's understanding.

### Pass 2 — Rhythm

- Read aloud. Mark every place you stumble.
- Check sentence-length variance per paragraph.
- Cut intensifiers, "suddenly," and filter words.

### Pass 3 — Dialogue

- Read only the dialogue. Can you identify the speakers?
- Mark every exchange without a transaction.
- Remove adverbs and said-bookisms.
- Check that at least one question per major conversation goes unanswered.

### Pass 4 — Description and figuration

- Highlight every simile. Ask what it does. Cut the ones that only decorate.
- Check that every place description maps power.
- Ensure at least three senses per scene.

### Pass 5 — Psychology

- Run the accuracy checklist from [`03-character-psychology.md`](03-character-psychology.md#14-accuracy-checklist).
- Check that every major character has a wound, a lie, and a cost.
- Check that the villain's behaviour is consistent with their psychology, and that the psychology is shown
  before it is explained.

### Pass 6 — Evidence

- Reconstruct the solution from the clues on the page. If you cannot, add the missing clue or change the
  solution.
- Check that no character knows something the reader has not been shown.
- Check that coincidences are earned or acknowledged.

### Pass 7 — Ethics

- Apply the four tests for violence and sex.
- Check that every victim is a person.
- Check that no character exists only to be harmed.
- Check that the ending's moral stance is deliberate.

## 4. The diagnostic interview

Answer these about your draft in one sentence each. If you cannot, the draft does not yet know.

1. What does my detective want in the first chapter, and what do they want by the last?
2. What is the lie my detective believes, and where does the plot test it?
3. Who pays for my detective's way of coping?
4. What is the antagonist's plan, in their own words?
5. Why does the victim matter to someone other than the detective?
6. What is the institutional obstacle, and who enforces it?
7. What is the false solution, and why is it plausible?
8. What does the reader know that the detective does not, and when?
9. What is the cost of the reveal?
10. What is the last image, and what does it answer?

## 5. A five-week reading plan

Read as a writer, with a pencil. One pair per week; read the older book first.

| Week | Pair | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | *Red Harvest* + *Killing Floor* | How does objective narration create character without interiority? |
| 2 | *The Big Sleep* + *The Galton Case* | How does the simile move from decoration to diagnosis? |
| 3 | *Cop Hater* + *Faceless Killers* | How does procedure become suspense, and how does it become social argument? |
| 4 | *A Rage in Harlem* + *L.A. Confidential* | How do comedy and atrocity coexist without cancelling each other? |
| 5 | *Postmortem* + *The Silence of the Lambs* | How does the professional gaze make horror credible — and when does it become voyeurism? |

For each pair, write 300 words answering the question with **two quotations** and **one sentence you can
use in your own work**.

## 6. Daily practice

A sustainable 20-minute routine:

- **5 minutes:** copy a paragraph from the canon by hand. Do not analyse it; feel the sentence lengths.
- **10 minutes:** write 150 words in a voice card you are studying.
- **5 minutes:** read aloud and cut three words.

Do this for thirty days and you will have a calibrated ear and 4,500 words of pastiche. The ear is the
point.

---

## 7. When you are stuck

- **Stuck on plot:** write the chapter from the antagonist's POV. They always know what happens next.
- **Stuck on voice:** write the scene as a police report, then translate it back into prose.
- **Stuck on a character:** give them objects — what they carry, what they hide, what they repair.
- **Stuck on a scene:** ask what the character wants in the next five minutes.
- **Stuck on the ending:** return to the first image. The ending is usually a reversal of it.
