# 06 · Structure and Plot: the architecture that carries the voice

Voice is what readers remember; structure is what keeps them reading. The canon offers five architectures,
each with its own promise to the reader, its own engine, and its own failure mode.

---

## 1. The five architectures

### 1.1 The PI investigation

**Series:** Marlowe, Archer, Warshawski, Millhone, Robicheaux.

**Promise:** a single consciousness will move through a social world and return with the truth.
**Engine:** the client's story is incomplete; each interview opens a new door; the detective's presence
changes the people he questions.
**Shape:** intake → interviews → a death or betrayal that raises the stakes → a false solution → the real
history → confrontation → elegy.
**Failure mode:** the plot becomes a sequence of interviews with no escalation; the detective is a passive
recorder.

**Fix:** make every interview **cost** something — a lie the detective has to tell, a relationship damaged,
a physical risk. Chandler's plots are famously loose, but his scenes are not: each one changes Marlowe's
position.

### 1.2 The procedural

**Series:** McBain, Sjöwall & Wahlöö, Connelly, Mankell, Rankin, Cornwell.

**Promise:** the reader will see how the work is actually done, and the solution will be earned by evidence.
**Engine:** procedure + contingency. A break, a file, a witness, a lab result; the case advances through
work and luck.
**Shape:** crime → scene → investigation → institutional obstacle → second crime or escalation → break →
confrontation → administrative aftermath.
**Failure mode:** procedure as filler; the case solved by a coincidence the book has not earned.

**Fix:** make the **institution** the antagonist as much as the criminal. The budget, the jurisdiction, the
press, the boss: these are the obstacles that make procedure dramatic.

### 1.3 The ensemble braid

**Series:** McBain, Sjöwall & Wahlöö, Ellroy, Rankin (secondary).

**Promise:** a world, not a hero. Multiple cases and lives will intersect.
**Engine:** juxtaposition. One case comments on another; a personal crisis mirrors a professional one.
**Shape:** 3–5 strands, each with its own arc; intersections at act breaks; a convergence that is thematic
rather than mechanical.
**Failure mode:** strands that never touch; a braid that is just alternating chapters.

**Fix:** each strand must answer a question posed by another. If the cases are unrelated, the book needs a
**theme** that makes the juxtaposition meaningful (McBain's urban contingency; Ellroy's institutional
corruption).

### 1.4 The inverted / dual narrative

**Series:** Harris (*Red Dragon*, *Silence*), Nesbø, Larsson, Cornwell (later).

**Promise:** the reader will know more than the detective, and that knowledge will be unbearable.
**Engine:** dramatic irony. Cut between investigator and predator, or between two investigators with
different access.
**Shape:** crime → investigation → predator POV → near miss → escalation → convergence → confrontation.
**Failure mode:** the predator's POV becomes more interesting than the investigation; the book glamourises
the killer.

**Fix:** give the predator a **cost** and a **limitation**. Harris's Lecter is caged; Dolarhyde is trapped
by his own fantasy. The predator's POV should increase dread, not admiration.

### 1.5 The conspiracy / dossier

**Series:** Ellroy, Larsson, Paretsky (institutional), Sjöwall & Wahlöö (political).

**Promise:** the crime is a symptom of a system, and the investigation will expose the system.
**Engine:** documents, archives, money, testimony. Each revelation re-frames the previous one.
**Shape:** small case → pattern → institutional resistance → escalation → the archive → public exposure or
private defeat.
**Failure mode:** info-dumps; a conspiracy so large it loses human scale.

**Fix:** anchor the conspiracy in **one victim** and **one document**. Larsson's first novel works because
the corporate conspiracy is also a family mystery and a single woman's disappearance.

## 2. The case spine

Every crime novel, regardless of architecture, needs a spine. The proportions below are a starting point,
not a law.

| Beat | % of book | Function | Test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Intake** | 0–10% | The case arrives; the detective's world is established | Does the reader know what is at stake for the detective, not just the victim? |
| **2. First contradiction** | 10–20% | The official story does not fit | Does the detective have a reason to care? |
| **3. Escalation** | 20–45% | New victims, new obstacles, new suspects | Does each new fact raise the cost? |
| **4. False solution** | 45–60% | A theory that feels right and is wrong | Is the false solution plausible on the evidence the reader has? |
| **5. Cost** | 60–75% | The detective loses something (a person, a belief, a job) | Is the loss specific and irreversible? |
| **6. Reveal** | 75–90% | The true history; the confrontation | Could the reader have reconstructed it? |
| **7. Aftermath** | 90–100% | The world after; the elegy or the joke | Does the ending comment on the beginning? |

### The spine's two failure points

- **The false solution is too weak.** If the reader has already dismissed it, the middle sags.
- **The reveal is not a re-frame.** A good reveal changes the meaning of earlier scenes, not just the name
  of the culprit.

## 3. Scene functions

A scene must do at least two of these. If it does only one, it is a candidate for merging.

1. **Advance the case** (new fact, new suspect, new obstacle).
2. **Reveal character** (a choice under pressure).
3. **Raise the stakes** (a threat, a deadline, a cost).
4. **Establish world** (institution, class, geography) — never alone.
5. **Plant or pay off** (a clue, a lie, an object).
6. **Change a relationship** (ally to suspect, stranger to accomplice).
7. **Provide relief** (comedy, domesticity, sex) — must still do one of the above.

### Scene template

```
POV:
PLACE / TIME:
WANT:
OBSTACLE:
TURN:
COST:
EXIT (image / line / decision):
PLANT:
PAYOFF:
```

## 4. Chapter architecture

### 4.1 The fixed architecture

Sjöwall & Wahlöö wrote ten novels of thirty chapters each — a single 300-chapter work, one chapter per
year of Swedish social history. The fixed unit gives the reader a rhythm and the writer a discipline.

**Use it when:** you are writing a series with a strong social or historical arc, and you want each book to
feel like an instalment of one argument.

### 4.2 The procedural chapter

Connelly and Mankell often build a chapter around one **unit of work**: a scene, a document, an interview,
a drive. The chapter ends when the unit is complete or when a new fact arrives. This creates a satisfying
sense of progress without artificial cliffhangers.

### 4.3 The propulsion chapter

Child uses very short chapters (sometimes 500–900 words) and ends many on a hard cut. The page-turn is
structural: the reader is always one beat away from a resolution.

**Use it when:** the book's pleasure is momentum, and the reader is meant to read in one sitting.

### 4.4 The Patterson boundary

James Patterson (outside this canon) pushed the propulsion model to two-page chapters and co-authored
outlines. It is a different craft: the chapter is a **unit of commercial pacing**, not a unit of scene.
The lesson is real — readers will turn pages for short chapters — but the cost is depth of voice.

**Rule:** do not use short chapters to compensate for scenes that do not turn. Fix the scene first.

## 5. Clue fairness and misdirection

The canon is not primarily a puzzle tradition, but the best crime novels still obey an **epistemology**: the
reader must be able to follow the evidence, even if they cannot solve the case.

### 5.1 Three rules of fair play

1. **The clue must be on the page before the solution.** It may be buried, misread, or dismissed, but it
   must be there.
2. **The detective's inference must be traceable.** If the detective knows something the reader does not,
   the book must show how.
3. **The lie must be motivated.** A suspect who lies to protect someone is different from a suspect who
   lies because the plot needs a red herring.

### 5.2 Misdirection techniques

- **The false genre signal** (Harris): the book teaches you to expect one kind of killer and then reveals
  another.
- **The structural withhold** (Nesbø): a name, a face, or a timeline is hidden by the chapter order.
- **The sympathetic suspect** (Macdonald): the reader wants the likable person to be innocent.
- **The institutional assumption** (Connelly, Sjöwall & Wahlöö): everyone assumes the case is closed, so no
  one looks.
- **The unreliable memory** (Burke, Rankin): the detective's own past is a source of error.

### 5.3 The Christie contrast

Christie is excluded from this canon, but she is the best teacher of **fair-play architecture**: every clue
is available, and the solution is a re-ordering of information the reader already has. A noir novel does
not need her symmetry, but it benefits from her discipline.

## 6. Exposition engines

From [`02-prose-and-syntax.md`](02-prose-and-syntax.md#7-exposition): document, interview, expert, memory,
montage, discovery.

### Placement rules

- **Early:** the reader needs the rules of the world. Use expert and document.
- **Middle:** the reader needs complication, not explanation. Use interview and discovery.
- **Late:** the reader needs the re-frame. Use memory and document.
- **Never:** a lecture from a character who has no reason to give it.

## 7. Endings

The canon offers four endings. Each is a moral stance.

### 7.1 Restoration

The case is solved; the institution functions; the detective returns to his life. **McBain, Connelly
(partly), Grafton.** The cost is visible but contained. This is the most commercial ending and the easiest
to make false; it requires at least one irreparable loss.

### 7.2 Elegy

The case is solved; the damage cannot be undone; the detective understands something about the past.
**Macdonald, Burke, Rankin, Mankell.** The ending is a lament, not a victory.

### 7.3 Doom

The protagonist is destroyed or compromised; the system wins. **Ellroy, noir proper, Himes's later books.**
This is the tragic ending, and it requires the reader to have cared about the character's choices, not just
their fate.

### 7.4 Ambiguity

The truth is known, but justice is not; the detective chooses what to do with the knowledge.
**Paretsky, Larsson (public exposure as partial justice), Harris (*Hannibal*).** The ambiguity must be
earned by the book's ethical structure, not used as a hedge.

### The final-image test

The last image should answer the first image. Chandler's novels often end with a return to a room or a
street; Macdonald's with a landscape; Connelly's with the city; Burke's with water. The image is the book's
argument in sensory form.

## 8. Series architecture

A series is not a long novel; it is a set of promises renewed.

### 8.1 The long arc

- **Aging:** Connelly's Bosch ages in real time; the body becomes a plot constraint. Rankin's Rebus ages
  too, and the series' melancholy deepens.
- **Recurring antagonist:** Rankin's Cafferty, Himes's adversaries, Nesbø's Svein Finne. The recurring
  antagonist is a mirror and a promise; use sparingly.
- **Status quo change:** a marriage, a death, a resignation, a move. Change the board every three or four
  books, or the series becomes static.
- **The case that rhymes with the wound:** each book's case should touch the protagonist's history without
  repeating it.

### 8.2 The standalone-in-series

Most series books must work for a new reader. The canon's solutions:

- **A self-contained case** with a clear beginning and end.
- **A minimal continuity** (a partner, a city, a habit) established in the first 20 pages.
- **One ongoing thread** that rewards returning readers without confusing new ones.

### 8.3 The series bible

For your own project, maintain:

```
SERIES PROMISE:        what every book guarantees
PROTAGONIST ARC:       what changes across the series, and how slowly
RECURRING CAST:        who appears, and what they want
INSTITUTION:           the rules, ranks, and politics
GEOGRAPHY:             the map the reader will learn
MOTIFS:                the images and rituals that recur
CONTINUITY LOCKS:      facts that cannot change (a death, a name, a date)
```

## 9. Plotting checklists

### Premise

- [ ] The case can be stated in one sentence with a **specific** victim and a **specific** detective.
- [ ] The detective has a personal stake that is not identical to the victim's.
- [ ] The antagonist has a plan that makes sense from inside their head.
- [ ] The setting is a system, not a backdrop.

### Middle

- [ ] Every 10,000 words, the question changes.
- [ ] The false solution is plausible.
- [ ] The detective loses something before the reveal.
- [ ] The institutional obstacle is active, not decorative.
- [ ] There are at least two scenes of aftermath.

### Reveal

- [ ] The reader could reconstruct the solution from clues on the page.
- [ ] The reveal re-frames at least two earlier scenes.
- [ ] The culprit's psychology is consistent with their behaviour.
- [ ] The victim remains a person in the explanation.

### Ending

- [ ] The last image answers the first.
- [ ] The cost is specific.
- [ ] The genre promise is kept (justice, truth, or its absence).
- [ ] The final line is not a rhetorical question.
