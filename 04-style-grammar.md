# 04 · Style Grammar: the operative rules

This is the bible proper. Everything here is a rule you can apply to a sentence, a scene, or a draft. Rules
are marked **HARD** (binding unless you can state the effect you are buying by breaking it) or **SOFT**
(default; break with a reason).

The rules are derived from the twenty series, but they are written for a contemporary writer. Where the
canon's practice is dated or unethical, the rule says so.

---

## 0. Ten prime directives

1. **Voice is a permission structure.** Before you write a page, write down what your narrator is allowed
   to do and what they are forbidden to do. (Chandler: similes yes, tidy plots no. Leonard: dialogue yes,
   adverbs no. Ellroy: fragments yes, transitions no.)
2. **Every paragraph must change something.** A fact, a position, a decision. Atmosphere alone is not a
   paragraph.
3. **The sentence is the unit of rhythm, not the paragraph.** Vary length deliberately.
4. **Dialogue is action.** If a conversation does not change the balance of power, cut it or give it a
   transaction.
5. **Description is selection.** One precise detail beats three adjectives.
6. **Interiority must be earned.** The deeper you go into a head, the more the plot must need it.
7. **Exposition arrives when a character needs it.** Never when the author does.
8. **Violence has aftermath.** Sex has a point of view. Neither is a reward.
9. **The reader must be able to follow the evidence.** Mystery is not confusion.
10. **If it sounds like writing, rewrite it.** (Leonard's rule, and the best single rule in the canon.)

## 1. Register

Choose one base register. You may add one texture, not three.

| Register | Permitted | Forbidden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Objective** | Behaviour, dialogue, physical detail | Interiority, simile, explanation of motive |
| **Lyric** | Image, rhythm, sensory accumulation, judgment | Decorative simile, beauty without threat |
| **Telegraphic** | Fragments, montage, alliteration, compression | Transitions, throat-clearing, unearned obscurity |
| **Dialogic** | Talk, subtext, idiolect, comedy | Adverbs, description, authorial commentary |
| **Procedural** | Ordered work, documents, expertise | Melodrama, coincidence, procedure without cost |
| **Gothic** | Sensory horror, free indirect access to the predator, excess | Gore without etiquette; cruelty as aesthetic |

**Exercise:** write your register's permissions in three lines and tape them to the monitor. If a sentence
violates them, either cut it or change the register deliberately.

## 2. Sentences

### HARD rules

1. **Vary sentence length within every paragraph.** At least one sentence under 8 words, at least one over
   20, in any paragraph of 100+ words — unless you are deliberately writing a monotone (Ellroy) or a
   chant (Himes).
2. **No sentence that exists only to transition.** "Meanwhile," "Later that day," "As it turned out" are
   usually cuts.
3. **One idea per sentence** in action scenes. Two ideas per sentence in reflection.
4. **Prefer the concrete subject and the active verb.** "The lab lost the sample" beats "the sample was
   lost by the lab."
5. **Do not stack more than two subordinate clauses** unless the accumulation is the effect (Burke,
   Chandler).
6. **No adverb doing the work of a verb.** "He walked quickly" → "He hurried" or better, "He was already
   at the door."
7. **No "suddenly."** Either the event is sudden in the syntax, or it is not sudden.
8. **Do not begin more than two consecutive sentences with the same word** unless you are using anaphora
   deliberately.

### SOFT rules

- **Fragments** are legal and useful. Use them for pressure, not for default.
- **The em dash** is legal. Use it for interruption and self-correction, not for decoration.
- **Semicolons** are legal in lyric and analytic registers; suspicious in hardboiled and dialogic ones.
- **Questions** in narration are a cheap suspense device. One per chapter, maximum.

### Before / after

> **Before:** He walked slowly into the room and looked around at all the furniture, which was old and
> dusty, and he felt a sense of unease.
>
> **After:** He stopped in the doorway. The furniture was old, the dust undisturbed except for one clean
> oval on the sideboard, where something had been removed. He did not go in.

The second version has rhythm, selection, and a fact that changes the scene. The first has a mood.

## 3. Paragraphs

### HARD rules

1. **Every 120–180 words, something must change.** Chandler's half-sheet principle.
2. **Open on a fact or an action.** Reflection can come second.
3. **Close on a judgment, an image, or a turn.** Not on a summary.
4. **One paragraph, one job.** If a paragraph is doing atmosphere, exposition, and character at once, it
   will do none of them well.
5. **No paragraph longer than 150 words in a thriller register** unless the length is the effect (a
   montage, an interior spiral, a lyric set piece).
6. **White space is punctuation.** In action sequences, shorter paragraphs; in reflection, longer ones.

### The five shapes (from [`02-prose-and-syntax.md`](02-prose-and-syntax.md#32-the-five-paragraph-shapes))

Action block · Tableau · Interview · Montage · Interior block.

**Diagnostic:** label each paragraph in a chapter with one of the five. If more than a third are "interior
block," the chapter is probably static. If none are, the book has no interior life.

## 4. Dialogue

### HARD rules

1. **"Said" is the default tag.** Use it, or use an action beat.
2. **No adverb on a dialogue tag.** Ever, unless the adverb contradicts the line for irony, and then once
   per book.
3. **No said-bookisms** ("he opined," "she retorted," "he chuckled") in a realistic register. They are
   permitted in comic or period registers, sparingly.
4. **Every exchange has a transaction.** What does each speaker want from the other? If the answer is
   "information," make it specific: a name, a time, a concession, an admission, a promise.
5. **Characters must not explain what both already know.** Cut the "as you know" clause.
6. **Interrupt.** Real conversation overlaps. Use dashes, not ellipses, for interruption.
7. **Do not answer every question.** The unanswered question is a suspense device.
8. **Profanity must be characterful, not decorative.** If it can be removed without changing the voice, it
   is decoration.
9. **Dialect is syntax and vocabulary, not phonetic spelling.** Leonard's rule: use it sparingly. Himes
   renders idiom through rhythm, not apostrophes.
10. **No accents spelled out.** "Y'all" is fine; "Ah'm a-gonna" is vaudeville.

### SOFT rules

- **Untagged dialogue** is fine when the voices are distinct. If the reader has to count back, tag it.
- **Action beats** can replace tags, but do not stack them; a character who lights a cigarette in every
  scene is a stage direction.
- **Monologue** is legal if it is a performance, a confession, or a manipulation — and if someone
  interrupts it.

### Before / after

> **Before:** "I didn't kill him," he said defensively, looking away nervously.
>
> **After:** "I didn't kill him."
>
> He looked at the door, then at the window, then at the door again.

The second version shows the lie through behaviour and lets the reader judge. The first tells us what to
think.

## 5. Description

### HARD rules

1. **Describe what the point-of-view character would notice.** A cop notices exits; a pathologist notices
   the body; a journalist notices the official story.
2. **One detail per person.** The frayed collar, the melted earring, the smile like a parking ticket.
3. **No weather opening** unless the weather is a character in the scene (Leonard's rule; Burke is the
   exception because weather is his metaphysics).
4. **No catalogue of furniture.** A room is a set of affordances and a set of secrets.
5. **Place is power.** Say who can afford to be there and what the architecture hides.
6. **No simile that only decorates.** Every comparison must do diagnostic work.
7. **Do not describe a character's appearance in detail unless another character is looking at them, and
   tell us why.**

### SOFT rules

- **Sensory range:** at least three senses across a scene, not all in one paragraph.
- **Sound and smell** are underused in crime fiction; use them.
- **Colour** is a cheap effect unless it is specific ("the colour of weak coffee," not "brown").

## 6. Interiority and point of view

### HARD rules

1. **Pick a POV level and keep it.** Camera, behaviourist, reported thought, free indirect, confessional,
   unreliable. Do not drift without a structural signal.
2. **A POV shift must be signposted** by a scene break, a chapter, or a consistent structural pattern
   (rotating chapters, alternating protagonists).
3. **Do not enter a character's head to explain what the reader has already inferred.** That is the most
   common interiority failure.
4. **The narrator's vocabulary must match the narrator.** A cop does not think "I felt a profound
   existential unease."
5. **Free indirect thought needs a clear host.** If the reader cannot tell whose thought it is, rewrite.

### The POV matrix

| POV | Best for | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First person, camera | Hardboiled, procedural | Flatness; reader knows only what narrator knows |
| First person, confessional | Lyric, psychological | Self-indulgence; voice overwhelms plot |
| Third limited | Procedural, thriller | Distance; interiority feels reported |
| Third rotating | Ensemble, conspiracy | Loss of intimacy; reader confusion |
| First + third mixed | Dual protagonists (Larsson, Connelly) | Tonal whiplash; must be structural |

### Tense

- **Past** is the default. Detection is retrospective.
- **Present** is justified by urgency, a countdown, or a narrator who does not know the end. Cornwell's
  shift is the canon's case study; it works best when the book is a chase.
- **Do not switch tense mid-scene** without a clear frame.

---

## 7. Exposition

### The six engines

Document · Interview · Expert · Memory · Montage · Discovery.

### HARD rules

1. **Never explain something to a character who already knows it.**
2. **Attach exposition to a want.** The reader tolerates information when a character needs it.
3. **Break exposition with resistance.** An interruption, a contradiction, a lie.
4. **One system per scene.** Do not explain the lab, the department, and the victim's family in the same
   paragraph.
5. **Prefer the specific number.** "1.2 million kronor on page sixty-one" is more convincing than "a large
   sum."
6. **If the exposition is longer than 300 words, someone must interrupt it.**

## 8. Scene and chapter

### The scene spine

Every crime scene should have:

1. **A want** (someone wants something).
2. **An obstacle** (someone or something refuses).
3. **A turn** (the situation changes).
4. **A cost or a consequence** (someone pays, or the next step is chosen).
5. **An exit** (an image, a line, a decision).

If a scene has no turn, it is a report. If it has no cost, it is filler.

### Chapter endings

Use one of the four: **discovery, threat, cut, image**. Not a rhetorical question.

### Chapter length

- Thriller propulsion: 800–1,500 words.
- Procedural immersion: 2,000–4,000 words.
- Ensemble/social: 3,000–5,000 words, multiple scenes.

Pick a rhythm and keep it; readers calibrate.

## 9. Violence and sex on the page

The canon is 18+. The purpose of this section is not to sanitise it but to make it **craftworthy**.

### 9.1 Four tests

1. **Whose eyes?** Graphic detail is licensed by point of view. A pathologist sees differently from a
   traumatised witness, and the prose should reflect that.
2. **What is the aftermath?** Bruising, shock, infection, insomnia, a case that collapses. If no one pays,
   the scene is pornography.
3. **Is the victim a person?** Before and after. If the only function is to be found, rewrite.
4. **What does the scene argue?** Violence should mean something in the book's moral system — not merely
   demonstrate the villain's cruelty or the hero's toughness.

### 9.2 Violence: practical rules

- **One precise detail beats a paragraph of gore.** Cornwell's smell of fruit in a warm car; Ellroy's
  melting earring.
- **Mechanism, then consequence.** Show how the body breaks, then show who has to deal with it.
- **Do not choreograph for admiration.** If the prose is enjoying the technique more than the cost, the
  reader will too, and the scene will stop being frightening.
- **Off-page is a choice, not a coyness.** Some of the most violent books in the canon (Simenon, Sjöwall &
  Wahlöö) are mostly off-page, and they are not less serious.
- **Do not use sexual violence as atmosphere.** Name the perpetrator, show the consequence, and give the
  survivor a life beyond the event.

### 9.3 Sex: practical rules

- **Sex is character, power, or consequence.** If it is a reward for the plot, cut it.
- **Whose desire is being rendered?** The camera should be inside a point of view with a stake, not
  hovering like a music video.
- **Consent is legible.** Not a legal disclaimer — a behaviour. Who moves, who hesitates, who can say no.
- **Aftermath matters.** The conversation after, the awkwardness, the lie, the tenderness.
- **Do not use another person's body as a description of the protagonist's mood.**

### 9.4 The 18+ contract

Adult fiction may go further than YA, but "adult" is not a licence for emptiness. The canon's most durable
books are explicit when the explicitness is **characterological** (Burke's shame, Ellroy's self-disgust,
Larsson's argument about power) and coy when it is not. The genre's readers are not shocked by bodies; they
are bored by bodies that do not belong to anyone.

## 10. Diction

### Registers

| Register | Lexicon | Rhythm | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plain | Common words, concrete nouns | Short, declarative | Procedural, action, dialogue |
| Lyric | Sensory, slightly elevated | Long, cumulative | Landscape, grief, memory |
| Bureaucratic | Institutional nouns, passive voice | Clotted, parenthetical | Documents, meetings, cover-ups |
| Vernacular | Slang, idiom, profanity | Fast, overlapping | Street, squad room, family |
| Literary | Abstract nouns, allusion | Periodic, balanced | Narrators with education (use sparingly) |

### Rules

- **Do not mix registers without a reason.** A bureaucratic memo in a lyric chapter is a deliberate
  dissonance; it should mean something.
- **Period slang must be checked.** Ellroy's 1950s and Rankin's 1980s are researched, not guessed.
- **Profanity:** match the character, not the author's comfort. A cop who swears constantly in one scene
  and never in another needs a reason.
- **Slurs:** if the period requires them, do not use them for shock. Show the harm, and do not let the
  narration endorse the usage. Himes and Ellroy are the canon's difficult cases; both are products of their
  time and both are still read critically.

## 11. Cliché blacklist

Cut or rewrite on sight. Each entry includes the cure.

| Cliché | Cure |
| --- | --- |
| "The rain lashed the window" | A specific sound or consequence of rain |
| "She was trouble" | A behaviour that causes trouble |
| "He had seen it all" | One thing he has not seen, or one thing he cannot forget |
| "The city never sleeps" | A specific street at a specific hour |
| "A chill ran down his spine" | A physical detail with a cause |
| "Her blood ran cold" | A sensory trigger and a reaction |
| "All hell broke loose" | The first concrete action |
| "He let out a breath he didn't know he'd been holding" | A different involuntary response |
| "Their eyes locked" | A specific gaze and what it avoids |
| "The smell of death" | The actual smell (see Cornwell) |
| "A single tear rolled down her cheek" | A behaviour that costs something |
| "Little did he know" | Cut the narrator's foreshadowing |
| "This time it was personal" | Show the personal stake in a scene |
| "The killer had made a mistake" | The mistake, on the page |
| "She was beautiful in a way that..." | One detail, no thesis |
| "He was a man of few words" | Let him use few words |
| "The clock was ticking" | A deadline with a consequence |
| "Everything changed" | The specific change |
| "He didn't believe in coincidences" | A coincidence the reader can judge |
| "Welcome to my world" | Cut |

## 12. Voice consistency tests

1. **The blind test.** Remove the character names from three passages. Can a reader tell which series they
   belong to? If not, the voice is generic.
2. **The register test.** Does the narration use words the narrator would use? A first-person cop should not
   sound like a critic.
3. **The dialogue test.** Read only the dialogue. Can you identify the speakers by rhythm and vocabulary?
4. **The rhythm test.** Read aloud. If you run out of breath in the same place twice, the syntax is wrong.
5. **The permission test.** Does the passage do anything the voice is supposed to forbid? If so, is it
   deliberate?
6. **The delete test.** Remove every third sentence. Does the scene still work? If yes, the deleted
   sentences were not doing enough.

## 13. Revision checklist

### Pass 1 — rhythm

- [ ] Sentence-length variance in every paragraph.
- [ ] No more than two consecutive sentences with the same opening.
- [ ] Read aloud; fix the places you stumble.
- [ ] Cut "suddenly," "very," "really," "just," "somewhat."

### Pass 2 — dialogue

- [ ] Every exchange has a transaction.
- [ ] No adverbs on tags.
- [ ] No said-bookisms.
- [ ] At least one unanswered question per major conversation.
- [ ] Idiolects are distinguishable.

### Pass 3 — description

- [ ] Every paragraph changes something.
- [ ] One detail per person; no catalogues.
- [ ] Place maps power.
- [ ] No decorative simile.

### Pass 4 — interiority

- [ ] POV level is consistent.
- [ ] No explanation of what the reader already inferred.
- [ ] The narrator's vocabulary matches the narrator.
- [ ] Free indirect thought has a clear host.

### Pass 5 — exposition

- [ ] Each exposition is attached to a want.
- [ ] No "as you know" clauses.
- [ ] Systems are explained through use, not lecture.
- [ ] Numbers are specific.

### Pass 6 — ethics

- [ ] Violence has aftermath.
- [ ] Victims are people.
- [ ] Sex has a point of view and a consequence.
- [ ] Trauma is specific, not atmospheric.
- [ ] No character exists only to be harmed.

### Pass 7 — evidence

- [ ] The reader could reconstruct the solution from clues on the page.
- [ ] Coincidences are earned or acknowledged.
- [ ] The detective's deductions are grounded in evidence the reader has seen.

## 14. House targets for the linter

These are the defaults used by `tools/style_lint.py` when no voice card is supplied. They describe a
contemporary adult crime novel in the **plain-procedural** register.

| Metric | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Narration mean sentence length (dialogue excluded) | 12–18 words |
| Narration sentence-length SD | ≥ 7 |
| Sentences under 8 words | 20–35% |
| Sentences over 30 words | 5–15% |
| Dialogue share of all words | 45–65% |
| Filter words (saw/felt/heard/noticed/realized) | < 6 per 1,000 words |
| Intensifiers (very/really/quite/just) | < 8 per 1,000 words |
| Adverb-bearing dialogue tags | 0 |
| Said-bookisms | 0 |
| Exclamation marks | < 2 per 1,000 words |
| Cliché hits | 0 |
| Flesch reading ease | 60–80 |

A voice card overrides these. Targets are derived from the card's dials with deliberately simple linear
mappings so that every verdict is auditable:

```
narration_mean_sentence = 7 + 2.0 × sentence_len     (±2.5)
narration_sd_sentence   = 3.5 + 0.65 × variance      (±1.5)
dialogue_pct            = 5 + 6.5 × dialogue         (±8)
similes_per_1000        = 0.45 × figuration          (±1.0)
sensory_per_1000        = 2 + 1.4 × description      (±8)
numbers_per_1000        = 1 + 1.2 × procedure         (±7)
```

A Chandler card will accept a higher figuration count and a lower dialogue share; a Leonard card will
demand more dialogue and fewer semicolons. Dialogue is excluded from the sentence-length metrics because
quoted speech obeys different syntax; it is measured separately as a share.

## 15. The one rule above the rules

**Every rule in this chapter is a description of an effect, not a virtue.**

If you break a rule and can say what the break buys — speed, shock, lyricism, alienation, comedy — you are
writing. If you break it because you did not notice, you are drafting. The difference between the canon and
its imitators is almost never talent. It is that the canon's writers knew which rules they were breaking
and why.
