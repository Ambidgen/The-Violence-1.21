# 08 · Sources and Glossary

## A note on evidence

This project distinguishes three kinds of claim:

1. **Textual observation** — a description of what a book does. These are my readings and can be argued
   with.
2. **Reported fact** — publication dates, book counts, sales estimates, awards. These come from the sources
   below and are cited where they appear.
3. **Editorial estimate** — the twelve dials and the working ranges. These are calibrated judgments, not
   measurements, and are labelled as such.

No copyrighted text is reproduced at length. Quotations are short, attributed, and used for criticism.
Pastiche passages are original prose written for this project.

## 1. Craft and criticism

### The canon's own statements

- **Raymond Chandler, "The Simple Art of Murder"** (Atlantic Monthly, 1944; revised 1950). The genre's
  foundational manifesto: the detective as a man of honour, the rejection of the puzzle, and the argument
  for "the American language."
  [PDF](https://losbarbarosny.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/the-simple-art-of-murder.pdf)
- **Elmore Leonard, "Ten Rules of Writing"** (*New York Times*, 2001; book 2007). The rules quoted in this
  bible: no weather openings, no prologues, "said" only, no adverbs on "said," few exclamation marks, no
  "suddenly," dialect sparingly, no detailed character descriptions, no long descriptions of places, leave
  out the parts readers skip. "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it."
  [Guardian summary](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2010/feb/24/elmore-leonard-rules-for-writers) ·
  [Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/21/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/)
- **James Ellroy, Paris Review interview (Art of Fiction No. 201)**. On compression, alliteration, and the
  "direct, shorter-rather-than-longer sentence."
  [Paris Review](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/5948/the-art-of-fiction-no-201-james-ellroy)
- **Ian Rankin**, interview on the need for "chunks of prose" if you want a three-dimensional character.
  [Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/arts-entertainment/books/features/ian-rankin-rewards-of-an-inspectors-remorse-9131335.html)
- **Lee Child**, on writing "the fast stuff slow and the slow stuff fast," and on third-person versus
  first-person. [Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/opinion-the-man-with-no-plot-how-i-watched-lee-child-write-a-jack-reacher-novel) ·
  [New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2016/11/14/how-jack-reacher-was-built) ·
  [Sam Leith on Reacher's syntax](https://www.writing.ie/resources/what-jack-reachers-fist-can-teach-writers-about-sentence-structure-by-sam-leith/)
- **Ed McBain**, on giving cops "head colds, first names, and homey dialogue."
  [Crime Fiction Lover](https://crimefictionlover.com/2014/09/cis-the-enduring-excellence-of-the-87th-precinct/)
- **Georges Simenon**, Paris Review interview on the *romans durs* and the Maigret books as sketch versus
  painting. [Glasgow Review of Books](https://glasgowreviewofbooks.com/2014/05/22/monsieur-simenon-has-locked-himself-in/)
- **Ross Macdonald**, "The Writer as Detective Hero" (1965), on Archer as "less a doer than a questioner, a
  consciousness in which the meanings of other lives emerge."
  [MWA New York](https://www.mwany.org/2017/10/i-cant-say-goodbye-to-ross-macdonald/)

### Studies and essays

- **William Marling, "The Short Stories"** (in *Dashiell Hammett*, ed. critical essays). The source for the
  measured sentence lengths in early Hammett: average ~13 words; fight scenes ~8; descriptive passages
  "run into flab" past 15. [eNotes](https://www.enotes.com/topics/dashiell-hammett/criticism/hammett-dashiell-79186/william-marling-essay-date-1983)
- **Philip Durham, "The Technique"** (1963). On Chandler's half-sheet method (125–150 words per unit, each
  containing a detail, description, or action) and his preference for "said."
  [eNotes](https://www.enotes.com/topics/raymond-chandler/criticism/criticism/philip-durham-essay-date-1963-2)
- **Jean-Patrick Manchette, "Toast to Dash"** (1980). On Hammett's behaviourist style as a style of
  distrust: "It speaks only of what appears." [Marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/manchette/1980/toast-dash.htm)
- **Story Grid, "Free Indirect Style"** — a close reading of Harris's movement from reported to unmarked
  thought in *The Silence of the Lambs*. [Story Grid](https://storygrid.com/373/)
- **Woody Haut / *Hard-Boiled* anthology** and the critical literature on noir versus hardboiled. For
  definitions, see the glossary below and [Noir fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noir_fiction),
  [Hardboiled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardboiled).

## 3. Glossary

**Anaphora** — repetition of a word or phrase at the start of successive clauses. Creates urgency.

**Asyndeton** — omission of conjunctions between clauses. Creates speed and staccato.

**Beat** — a unit of scene action or dialogue; also a pause in a line.

**Close third** — third-person narration limited to one character's perceptions and thoughts.

**Complex trauma** — repeated, prolonged trauma (often interpersonal or institutional) with effects on
emotion regulation, self-concept, and relationships.

**Continuity lock** — a fact in a series that cannot change without breaking the reader's trust (a death, a
name, a date).

**Cozy** — a mystery subgenre with low violence, amateur sleuths, and a restored social order. Excluded from
this canon.

**Diegetic** — belonging to the story world (as opposed to the narration's commentary).

**Dissociation** — disruption of consciousness, memory, identity, or perception, often as a response to
trauma; ranges from spacing out to depersonalisation.

**Dossier architecture** — a structure built from documents, reports, and testimony, letting the reader
reconstruct the case (Larsson).

**Ellipsis** — a gap in narrative time; also the punctuation mark. In crime fiction, the strategic gap is a
plot device.

**Epistrophe** — repetition at the end of successive clauses. Creates closure and menace.

**Filter word** — a verb that reports perception rather than rendering it (*saw, heard, felt, noticed,
realized, watched, seemed*). Overuse creates distance.

**Focalisation** — the perspective through which the story is filtered (Genette). Close focalisation is the
default in crime fiction.

**Free indirect style** — third-person narration that takes on a character's thoughts and vocabulary without
quotation marks or reporting tags.

**Fridging** — killing or harming a character (historically a woman) solely to motivate the protagonist.

**Hardboiled** — a style of crime fiction defined by tough, cynical prose, a corrupt world, and a
protagonist with a personal code. Hammett and Chandler are the founders.

**Half-sheet method** — Chandler's practice of writing on half-sheets of 125–150 words, each containing a
detail, description, or action that advances the scene.

**Hypotaxis** — subordination of clauses (*because, although, which*). Creates analysis and irony.

**Idiolect** — a character's individual speech pattern: vocabulary, rhythm, tics, and evasions.

**Inverted detective story** — a story in which the culprit is known and the suspense is in the
investigation or the pursuit (Harris, noir proper).

**Moral injury** — distress following acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs; marked by guilt,
shame, betrayal, and withdrawal.

**Noir** — crime fiction centred on protagonists who are victims, suspects, or perpetrators, often
self-destructive, in a world without restoration. Related to but distinct from hardboiled.

**Parataxis** — coordination of clauses with little subordination (*and, then, so*). Creates flatness and
speed.

**Pastiche** — original writing that imitates a style or voice, usually as an exercise or homage.

**PCL-R** — the Hare Psychopathy Checklist-Revised, a clinical assessment with interpersonal, affective,
lifestyle, and antisocial facets.

**Police procedural** — a subgenre focused on the process of investigation and the institution that performs
it.

**Polysyndeton** — deliberate overuse of conjunctions. Creates a tidal, cumulative effect.

**Register** — the level and texture of language appropriate to a narrator, character, or scene (plain,
lyric, bureaucratic, vernacular, literary).

**Roman dur** — Simenon's term for his non-Maigret "hard novels": psychological, fatalistic, often without a
detective.

**Said-bookism** — a dialogue tag other than "said" used for colour (*opined, retorted, chuckled*).

**Self-medication** — use of substances or behaviours to manage unbearable affect; a common model for
addiction in crime fiction.

**Series bible** — a continuity document for a series: promise, arc, cast, institution, geography, motifs,
locks.

**Telegraphese** — a compressed, staccato prose style that omits articles, conjunctions, and transitions
(Ellroy).

**Triarchic model** — a model of psychopathy with three dimensions: boldness, meanness, and disinhibition.

**Voice** — the sum of a narrator's syntax, diction, rhythm, permissions, and prohibitions; not a mood.

## 4. Translation note

Simenon, Sjöwall & Wahlöö, Mankell, Larsson, and Nesbø are studied in English translation. Their sentence
rhythms are partly the translators'. Where this matters, the series cards say so. The most conspicuous case
is Simenon: his famous "flat" prose in English is the joint product of his restricted French vocabulary and
his translators' choices. The craft lesson — restraint, ordinary detail, atmosphere through accumulation —
survives translation; the exact cadence may not.
