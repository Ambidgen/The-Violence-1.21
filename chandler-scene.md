The elevator had stopped working sometime in the afternoon and nobody had thought to tell the tenants, so
I walked up four flights with the kind of patience a man develops when he has nowhere else to be and no one
waiting for him there. The corridor smelled of carpet shampoo and old cigarettes, and the light over the
stairwell had the yellow, exhausted look of a thing that had been on since before the war and had stopped
expecting anyone to turn it off.

She was standing outside my door with a manila envelope held against her chest like a hymn book.

"You're Marlowe," she said.

"I'm what's left of him at this hour."

"I've been waiting forty minutes."

"That's the building's fault, not mine. The elevator's been dead since Tuesday and the stairs don't take
messages."

She was tall and she was careful, and the careful ones were always the ones who had already decided what
they were going to say and had rehearsed it in the car. She wore a dark coat with the collar turned up, and
her gloves were the thin kind that cost more than the coat, and she did not take them off to shake my hand.

"You come recommended," she said.

"By who?"

"By somebody who doesn't want his name in it."

"That's the best kind of recommendation and the worst kind of client."

She smiled at that, but it was a smile she had practised, and it went out the way a match goes out when
there's nothing left to burn.

"May I come in?"

"You may. The furniture's not much and the whiskey's not good, but the door locks."

I unlocked it and stood aside, and the phone on the desk started ringing before she was through the
doorway, which is the way the city works: it waits until you have somebody in the room and then it calls to
remind you what you owe it.
