# 19 · Lee Child — Jack Reacher

> **Card** · 1997–present · 31 novels (as of late 2026) · action thriller · 3rd person limited (five in 1st person) / past ·
> Contemporary USA
> **Read first:** *Killing Floor* (1997)
> **Popularity:** 100+ million copies; 29 novels; two films and a hit TV series; a defining commercial
> thriller voice.
> **Voice card:** sentence 2 · variance 4 · figuration 1 · interiority 2 · dialogue 4 · description 2 ·
> violence 6 · irony 4 · fatalism 4 · procedure 5 · sex 2 · distance 8

## What the series solved

Child solved **propulsion**. His prose is engineered to keep the reader moving: short sentences, plain
words, minimal punctuation, and a narrator who reports rather than reflects. The style performs the
character — Reacher is a man who strips away the unnecessary, and the sentences do the same.

He also solved **the competent stranger**. Reacher arrives, observes, measures, and acts. The pleasure is
not suspense about whether he will win; it is watching a professional solve a problem efficiently. Child's
own formulation of the pacing principle is the best one-line craft lesson in the canon: *write the fast
stuff slow and the slow stuff fast.* Fights are slowed to choreography; travel and waiting are compressed.

The repetition is deliberate. A phrase recurring is not a failure of vocabulary; it mimics the way a mind
checks and rechecks. The prose is a cognitive instrument.

## Syntax and prose mechanics

- **Sentence.** Short, declarative, often verbless. Fragments are normal. The average is well below the
  genre norm, and the variance is controlled rather than wild.
- **Paragraph.** Often one or two sentences. White space is part of the rhythm.
- **Dialogue.** Sparse and functional. Reacher asks a question, gets an answer, and the narration notes
  *how* it was delivered — too fast, too slow, a half-second late — without explaining what it means.
- **Description.** Measured, not pictorial. A room is described by exits, distances, and what is within
  reach. People are described by size and movement.
- **Figuration.** Almost none. Child treats metaphor as a delay.
- **Punctuation.** Minimal. Periods, commas, occasional dashes. Very few semicolons; almost no exclamation
  marks.
- **Exposition.** Through Reacher's arithmetic and through dialogue. The reader is trusted to follow the
  calculation.

## Character psychology

**Reacher** is a fantasy of **competence and freedom**. He has no possessions, no address, no dependents,
and no fear that is not tactical. His psychology is deliberately opaque: the reader gets his calculations,
not his confessions. The wound — a military childhood, a brother killed, a career ended — is present but
rationed.

The series' appeal is not that Reacher is realistic but that his **code** is legible: he protects the weak,
punishes bullies, and refuses to be bought. He is a western hero in a contemporary America, and the books
are honest about the pleasure of that fantasy.

**Antagonists** are bullies with power: a town's ruling family, a private militia, a corrupt business. They
are usually defeated by a combination of arithmetic and violence.

**Supporting characters** are often ordinary people who become competent under Reacher's example. The
series is oddly optimistic about the decency of strangers.

## Signature devices

1. **Short declaratives.** Cut every sentence that can be cut.
2. **Repetition as thought.** Repeat a phrase to show the mind working.
3. **Measurement.** Describe space by distance, time, and threat.
4. **Fast stuff slow.** Slow the violence into choreography; compress the waiting.
5. **The unstated inference.** Report the behavioural tell; do not explain what it means.

## What to steal / what to avoid

**Steal:** propulsion; the discipline of plain words; the confidence to leave inference to the reader.

**Avoid:** mistaking simplicity for ease. Child's sentences are short but not random; the rhythm is
controlled, and the details are selected. Imitators produce prose that is merely thin, with characters who
are merely capable.

## Diagnostic passage (original, in the voice)

> The bus stopped at four in the afternoon. No one got off. I got off.
>
> The town was two streets and a gas station. The gas station was closed. The sign said OPEN, but the door
> was locked and the lights were off, which meant either the owner was careless or the owner was dead.
>
> I stood on the sidewalk and counted. Six cars. Three pickups. One of the pickups had mud on the tyres and
> clean windows, which is backwards. A man came out of the diner and looked at me for two seconds longer
> than a man looks at a stranger.
>
> I went into the diner. There were four tables and a counter and a clock that was seven minutes slow. I sat
> at the counter and ordered coffee. The waitress poured it and did not ask what I wanted to eat. That was
> the second thing.

**Annotation**

- Sentences are short, some verbless. The paragraph breaks do the pacing.
- The narrator reports measurements and inferences, not feelings. The inference is left to the reader.
- "Which is backwards" is the only evaluation, and it is practical, not emotional.
- The ending withholds: "the second thing" implies a list, which creates forward pressure.

## Exercise

Write a 400-word arrival scene. Constraints: sentences averaging under 12 words; no metaphor; at least four
numerical observations; one behavioural tell reported but not explained; no interiority beyond inference;
end mid-investigation.
