# 09 · One Scene, Five Ways

This chapter is the bridge between the analysis and the writing. It takes a single, deliberately plain
scene — the same events, the same facts, the same witness — and renders it five times: once neutral, once in
each of four registers. Nothing in the plot changes. Everything about the reading experience changes.

The scene:

> A detective arrives at a motel at two in the morning. The room is open. The manager says he saw nothing.
> The bed is made. A suitcase is open on the floor. A toothbrush is wet. There is a coat in the closet that
> does not belong to the missing woman. Outside, a car starts.

That is a few hundred characters of information and eight facts. Every rendering below distributes the
same eight, and no rendering adds a ninth.

---

## 1. The neutral draft

> Detective Mara Vance reached the Starlite Motel at 2:10 a.m. The door of Room 14 was standing open, and
> the manager, whose name was Purdy, came out into the parking lot in a bathrobe and told her he had not
> seen anyone all night. Vance went in. The bed was made. An open suitcase lay on the floor, and a
> toothbrush on the bathroom sink was still wet. In the closet she found a woman's coat that did not belong
> to the missing tenant. Behind her, in the lot, a car started.

This is competent and dead. It reports. It commits to nothing — not to Vance's temperament, not to Purdy's
nerves, not to the meaning of a wet toothbrush. It is the draft you write to find out what happens, and it
is the draft the other four are revisions of.

Note what the neutral draft already gets right, because all four variants keep it: the facts are ordered by
**discovery**, not by chronology, and the last fact arrives from outside the room.

---

## 2. Objective camera

> At 2:10 the Starlite's vacancy sign was off. Room 14 stood open. Purdy came out of the office in a
> bathrobe, holding the belt, and said nobody had come through the lot since eleven.
>
> The bed was made. A suitcase lay open on the carpet, one strap cut. The toothbrush on the sink was wet.
>
> In the closet, a coat. Size six. Not the tenant's.
>
> In the lot a car started. Purdy looked at the lot. Then at the door. Then at the lot.

**What it does.** No interiority, no judgments, no similes. The camera records; the reader interprets. The
shortest sentence in the passage — "In the closet, a coat." — is also the most violent, because the
fragment withholds the verb that would tell us how to feel.

**The psychology is in the blocking.** Purdy looks at the lot, then the door, then the lot again. Nothing is
said about his guilt or his fear. The repetition is the confession-shaped hole.

**Syntax:** narration mean 6–9 words, SD 4–6, 70–80% of sentences under eight words, dialogue 0%, figuration
near zero. **Use for:** Hammett,
Leonard at his coldest, the opening third of a procedural.

**Failure mode:** without a moral center, this becomes a police report. Hammett earns the camera by giving
the Op a code the camera never states; if your detective has no code, the flatness reads as indifference.

---

## 3. Lyric interior

> The Starlite had been dying since the Eisenhower administration, and the vacancy sign had finally given
> up the argument. Room 14 stood open the way a mouth is open.
>
> Purdy came out in a bathrobe with the belt trailing, a man who had been awake for hours and wanted me to
> believe that a parking lot had kept him informed. "Nobody through the lot since eleven," he said, and
> looked at the lot as if it might contradict him.
>
> Inside, the bed was made with the awful neatness of a room nobody had slept in. The suitcase lay open on
> the carpet, one strap cut, and on the sink a toothbrush was still wet and the water in the bowl was still
> moving.
>
> The coat in the closet was size six, and it was not hers. I stood there a while, holding it, learning the
> shape of somebody else's bad night. In the lot, a car started, and Purdy's eyes went to it the way a
> man's eyes go to a door he has already decided not to open.

**What it does.** The narration judges. "The awful neatness of a room nobody had slept in" is a fact plus a
verdict; the verdict is the voice. Two comparisons carry emotional weight ("open the way a mouth is open",
"the way a man's eyes go to a door"), and both are negative in form — they describe what something is like
by describing the shape of a mistake.

**The psychology is in the narrator's attention.** Marlowe-Archer narrators notice what they are afraid of.
The coat gets a whole sentence of holding, and the holding is the characterization.

**Syntax:** narration mean 18–24 words, almost no sentences under eight, dialogue 5–15%, and roughly one
comparison every 80–100 words — which in this passage means two, both of them invisible to the linter (see
"On the proxies" below).
**Use for:** Chandler, Macdonald, Burke (with more weather and more God), Rankin in his reflective mode.

**Failure mode:** the metaphors start explaining the emotions they were hired to replace. If every object
arrives with a verdict attached, the reader stops doing any work and the prose becomes a lecture with
scenery.

---

## 4. Telegraphic montage

> 2:10. Starlite. Vacancy off.
>
> Room 14 open. Purdy, bathrobe, belt in hand. "Nobody since eleven." He said it to the lot.
>
> Bed made. Suitcase open. Strap cut.
>
> Sink: toothbrush, wet.
>
> Closet: coat. Size six. Not hers.
>
> Lot: car starting. Purdy's eyes: lot, door, lot.
>
> Six facts. One of them was lying.

**What it does.** Ellroy's engine: the paragraph is not a sentence but a **unit of information**, and the
white space between units is where suspense lives. The colon replaces the verb; the fragment replaces the
clause; the last line replaces the narrator's judgment with a summary that is also an accusation.

**The psychology is in the count.** "Six facts. One of them was lying." Nobody in the scene is described as
frightened; the *form* is paranoid. This is how Ellroy gets inside damaged institutions without a
psychologist's vocabulary.

**Syntax:** 3–5 words per unit, SD 1–3, dialogue 20–30% (short bursts), semicolons 0, colons many, and
paragraphs of one to three lines.
**Use for:** Ellroy, late Child in action sequences, any chapter that needs to feel like a raid.

**Failure mode:** monotony. If everything is a fragment, nothing is. The form only works when it interrupts
something longer — a lyrical paragraph, a stretch of ordinary procedure — and the interruption is the point.

## 5. Dialogic engine

> "You the manager?"
>
> "Purdy. I already talked to the other one."
>
> "Talk to me."
>
> "Nobody came through the lot since eleven. I'd of seen them. I was up."
>
> "You were up."
>
> "I was up."
>
> Vance looked at the open door. "This how you leave a room when nobody's in it?"
>
> "The door was open when I came out. That's what I'm telling you."
>
> Inside: bed made, suitcase open, strap cut. She came back out with the coat over her arm.
>
> "Size six," she said. "Your tenant a six?"
>
> Purdy looked at the coat. Then at the lot. "I don't know what size she is."
>
> "You know what size the coat is."
>
> A car started at the far end of the lot. Purdy didn't turn around. That was the part she would remember.

**What it does.** Leonard's method: the scene is an exchange, and the narration exists only to record what
cannot be said. Character is revealed through **what people choose to answer**, not what they are asked.
Purdy answers a question about the door with a story about himself; Vance hears the evasion and does not
name it.

**The psychology is in the evasion.** The reader learns Purdy is frightened because he defends himself
before he is accused. Nothing is asserted about his inner state.

**Syntax:** narration mean 6–9 words, dialogue 45–60%, figuration near zero, said-bookisms zero, and
paragraphs that break at every turn of the exchange.
**Use for:** Leonard, McBain's squad-room scenes, Rankin's interviews, any scene where two people are lying
to each other politely.

**Failure mode:** talking heads. The dialogue must keep changing the physical situation — a coat lifted, a
door looked at, a car starting — or the scene becomes a transcript.

## The measurements

Each passage was pasted into a plain text file and measured with the repository's linter. The differences
stop being impressions:

| Variant | Words | Narration mean | Narration SD | Sentences <8w | Dialogue | Words / paragraph |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Neutral draft | 94 | 15.7 | 14.6 | 33% | 0% | 94.0 |
| Objective camera | 85 | 6.5 | 5.0 | 77% | 0% | 21.2 |
| Lyric interior | 179 | 19.2 | 7.3 | 0% | 3% | 44.8 |
| Telegraphic montage | 52 | 2.9 | 1.4 | 94% | 6% | 7.4 |
| Dialogic engine | 132 | 6.1 | 2.6 | 83% | 58% | 10.2 |

Read the table as a set of trade-offs rather than a scoreboard.

- **SD is not virtue.** The neutral draft has the highest sentence-length variance of the five (14.6) and is
  the worst writing. One 40-word sentence among five short ones produces a big number and a monotonous
  paragraph. Variance is only expressive when the *distribution* is deliberate — long, long, short, long.
- **Paragraph length is a dial the sentence metrics hide.** The neutral draft averages 94 words per
  paragraph; the objective camera 21; the telegraphic variant 7.4. That single number explains most of the
  difference in reading speed between Hammett and Ellroy — and it is invisible if you only count sentences.
- **Dialogue and figuration are rivals.** Every point of dialogue share pushes the narrator out of the room,
  and the narrator is where the comparisons live. The dialogic variant is at 58% dialogue and near-zero
  figuration because the two compete for the same words.
- **Short sentences are cheap to write and expensive to sustain.** The telegraphic variant reaches 94% of
  sentences under eight words in 52 words. At chapter length, that rate becomes a mannerism unless it is
  broken by longer units.

### On the proxies

Two honest caveats, both visible in this table:

1. The simile counter looks for `like a`, `like the`, and `as … as`. The lyric passage contains two
   comparisons — "open the way a mouth is open" and "the way a man's eyes go to a door" — and the linter
   scores it **0.0**. The metric is a floor, not a count.
2. The sensory lexicon is small and literal. The lyric passage scores *lower* on it than the objective
   camera, which is nonsense as literary judgment and a useful reminder of what a wordlist can and cannot
   see.

Use the numbers to notice your habits, not to certify your prose. If a metric disagrees with a careful
reader, the reader is right.

## The exercise

1. Write a neutral draft of a scene you already know: 100–150 words, eight facts, one discovery order.
2. Rewrite it four times, once per register, **without changing the facts**.
3. Measure all five. Note which variant is furthest from its own target — that is the one you were
   imitating rather than writing.
4. Rewrite the weakest variant once more, changing one dial by two points only (e.g. dialogue 4 → 6).
5. Keep the version that survives being read aloud.

**The rule this chapter teaches:** voice is not a vocabulary. It is a set of decisions about what to put in
the sentence, what to leave outside it, and how long to make the reader wait.
