# 02 · Raymond Chandler — Philip Marlowe

> **Card** · 1939–1958 · 7 novels · hardboiled noir · 1st person / past · Los Angeles
> **Read first:** *The Big Sleep* (1939)
> **Popularity:** seven novels, never out of print, repeatedly filmed, and the standard against which
> literary crime prose is still measured.
> **Voice card:** sentence 6 · variance 8 · figuration 8 · interiority 8 · dialogue 6 · description 6 ·
> violence 5 · irony 8 · fatalism 7 · procedure 3 · sex 4 · distance 3

## What the series solved

Chandler took Hammett's behaviourism and added a **narrator with a soul**. Marlowe observes like a
detective and reacts like a moralist, and the gap between those two activities is the music of the books.
He is also the first crime writer to make the *simile* a structural device rather than a decoration: the
comparisons are how the narrator judges a city that has no other way of being judged.

Chandler's famous method was physical. He wrote on half-sheets of roughly 125–150 words and required each
half-sheet to contain a detail, a description, or an action that advanced the scene. That is why his
paragraphs feel both dense and fast: every unit of the page is doing work, and the work is visible.

## Syntax and prose mechanics

- **Sentence.** Extreme alternation. A long, clause-stacked sentence establishes a mood; a four-word
  sentence detonates it. The rhythm is closer to stand-up comedy than to journalism: setup, setup, punch.
- **Paragraph.** Often opens with a flat statement of fact, turns through one or two observations, and
  closes on an image or a joke that re-frames everything before it.
- **Dialogue.** Fast, overlapping, full of deflection. People answer the question they prefer rather than
  the one asked. Marlowe's wisecracks are not decoration; they are how he refuses to be recruited into
  other people's self-pity.
- **Description.** Sensory but selective: light, smell, the quality of a room's silence. Chandler is
  excellent on interiors because interiors are where money hides.
- **Figuration.** The signature. Two rules govern it: the comparison must be **incongruous** (a tarantula
  on angel food), and it must be **morally diagnostic** (it tells you something about the world being
  described, not just the writer's cleverness).
- **Punctuation.** Commas, dashes, and the occasional semicolon; the dash is used for self-interruption,
  which is a character trait.
- **Exposition.** Often deliberately muddy — Chandler admitted that the plot of *The Big Sleep* had loose
  ends he could not resolve. The scene, not the solution, is the unit of value.

> "It was about eleven o'clock in the morning, mid October, with the sun not shining and a look of hard
> wet rain in the clearness of the foothills." — *The Big Sleep*

Note the negative construction ("not shining") and the abstract noun ("clearness") doing concrete work.
Chandler's lyricism is almost always built out of *absences*.

## Character psychology

**Marlowe** is a knight in a cheap suit: a man with a code he never states and cannot abandon. His
psychology is organised around **loneliness and refusal**. He is offered money, sex, friendship, and
career advancement, and he declines all of them in ways that cost him. His wit is a defence against
being absorbed by other people's corruption.

Crucially, Marlowe is not invulnerable. He gets beaten, drugged, jailed, and humiliated, and the books
register the physical cost. The code is not a superpower; it is a stubborn injury.

**Antagonists** are usually not masterminds but **respectable people protecting a story**. The Sternwoods,
the Grayles, the wealthy families: the crime is the maintenance of reputation. Chandler's villains are
often less evil than the system that makes their evil profitable.

**Women** in the canon are a problem the series partly diagnoses and partly reproduces. The femmes fatales
are intelligent and trapped; the good women are often sentimentalised. A modern pastiche must keep the
intelligence and refuse the Madonna/whore sorting.

## Signature devices

1. **The incongruous simile.** Compare the thing to something from a different tax bracket, species, or
   industry. It must reveal power.
2. **The negative description.** Define a place by what is missing: no sun, no noise, no one at the desk.
3. **Rhythmic repetition.** Repeat a word from the end of one sentence at the start of the next to create
   acceleration.
4. **The wisecrack as refusal.** A joke that declines an emotional invitation.
5. **Scene over plot.** Build set pieces that could stand alone; connect them with the detective's body.

## What to steal / what to avoid

**Steal:** the long/short rhythm; the simile that judges; the narrator who is wounded by his own code.

**Avoid:** simile as performance. Chandler's best comparisons are load-bearing; his imitators' are
wallpaper. Also avoid inherited gender politics and the plot that dissolves under questioning — those are
period failures, not features.

## Diagnostic passage (original, in the voice)

> I had been in the office since eight, which was three hours longer than the job warranted and one hour
> longer than my temper. The morning came through the frosted glass the colour of weak coffee, and the fan
> turned its head from side to side like a man who had heard the argument before and had stopped taking
> sides. She walked in without knocking, which is the kind of thing that either means money or means
> trouble, and in Los Angeles it usually means both. She wore a grey suit that had been tailored somewhere
> the sun doesn't reach. Her smile was as warm as a parking ticket.

**Annotation**

- The first sentence is 24 words and coils; the last is 9 and lands. The variation is the voice.
- Two similes, both negative in temperature: the fan as a bored man, the smile as a ticket. Each tells you
  something about the narrator's view of the world, not just about the object.
- "which is the kind of thing that…" is a Chandler cadence: a parenthetical generalisation that pretends to
  be casual and is actually the thesis.
- The paragraph ends on a judgement disguised as a joke. That is how Marlowe says "I don't trust her."
