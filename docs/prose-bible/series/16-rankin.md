# 16 · Ian Rankin — John Rebus

> **Card** · 1987–present · 25+ novels · Tartan noir / procedural · 3rd person limited, with ensemble
> shifts / past · Edinburgh
> **Read first:** *Knots and Crosses* (1987) or *The Black Book* (1993)
> **Popularity:** a dominant UK bestseller for three decades; Rebus is a national cultural figure; two TV
> series and a persistent presence in British crime criticism.
> **Voice card:** sentence 6 · variance 7 · figuration 5 · interiority 7 · dialogue 7 · description 6 ·
> violence 5 · irony 6 · fatalism 5 · procedure 6 · sex 4 · distance 5

## What the series solved

Rankin solved **the city as a class system**. Edinburgh in these books is not a postcard; it is a vertical
map of money, power, and concealment. The Old Town and New Town, the schemes and the clubs, the parliament
and the pub: Rebus moves through all of it because a policeman has a right of entry to both the underworld
and the overworld.

He also solved **the compromised detective**. Rebus is not corrupt in the ordinary sense, but he is
complicit: he has cut corners, protected people he should not have, and made peace with a version of
justice that fits his temperament. The series is interested in what that costs over decades.

Structurally, Rankin is a bridge between the ensemble procedural and the literary novel. He gives Rebus
long blocks of interior monologue — a deliberate choice, as he has said, because "if you want a
three-dimensional character, you need chunks of prose."

## Syntax and prose mechanics

- **Sentence.** Medium-to-long, with a strong conversational swing. Rankin's prose has been described as
  "constantly flirting with ragged harmonies," which is accurate: it is deliberately untidy in a controlled
  way.
- **Paragraph.** Alternates between dialogue scenes and long interior blocks. The interior blocks are where
  the case is actually solved, because Rebus thinks by association.
- **Dialogue.** Dry, fast, Scottish-inflected without phonetic spelling. Banter is a form of affection and
  a form of evasion.
- **Description.** Weather, streets, closes, stairwells, pubs. Rankin is excellent on the physical
  experience of a city in bad weather.
- **Figuration.** Moderate; images are often architectural or musical.
- **Punctuation.** Conventional, with dashes for interruption.
- **Exposition.** Through conversation, files, and Rebus's associative memory. The reader is trusted to
  follow the geography.

## Character psychology

**Rebus** is defined by **obsession and guilt**. He is a difficult man: heavy drinker, bad at relationships,
incapable of leaving the job alone. His psychology is organised around a case he cannot quite close and a
city he cannot quite leave. He is not a wounded hero in the spectacular sense; he is a man who has made
his wound into a vocation.

The series' great relationship is with **Morris Cafferty**, the gangster. They are opponents who understand
each other better than either understands his own side. That mutual recognition is the series' deepest
psychological insight: the cop and the criminal are both products of the same city and the same class
system.

**Siobhan Clarke** is the necessary counterweight: younger, more professional, more adaptable, and
ultimately the person who inherits the method without the self-destruction.

## Signature devices

1. **The dual city.** Describe the same place at two levels of money and power.
2. **Interior monologue as detection.** Let the detective think associatively through music, memory, and
   geography.
3. **The antagonist as mirror.** Give the detective a criminal who understands him.
4. **Dry banter as intimacy.** Let affection appear as insult.
5. **Real time.** Set each book in the year it is written; let the world change around the characters.

## What to steal / what to avoid

**Steal:** the class geography; the long interior block that actually advances the case; the mirror
antagonist.

**Avoid:** tourist Edinburgh; a monologue that becomes an essay; nostalgia for a city that never existed.
Rankin's best books are angry about inequality, not sentimental about cobblestones.

## Diagnostic passage (original, in the voice)

> The flat was on the fourth floor of a building that had been respectable when the street had been
> respectable, which was roughly the same year the heating had been installed. Rebus climbed slowly,
> stopping on the second landing to look out the window at the back court. There were two wheelie bins, a
> bicycle missing its front wheel, and a man in a dressing gown smoking a cigarette with the concentration
> of someone performing a difficult operation.
>
> The door at the top of the stairs was open. Inside, a woman was sitting on the floor with her back
> against the sofa, holding a mug in both hands as if it were keeping her in the room.
>
> "You'll be the police," she said.
>
> "I'll be somebody," Rebus said, and stepped inside.

**Annotation**

- The first sentence is a social history disguised as a joke about heating. The class map is in the
  architecture.
- The man in the dressing gown is a Rankin detail: comic, specific, and faintly menacing.
- The dialogue is a deflation. Rebus's answer is evasive and affectionate at once.
- The interiority is minimal here, but the pacing — slow climb, observation, threshold — is how Rankin
  builds a scene before the monologue arrives.

## Exercise

Write a 450-word scene in which a detective climbs to a flat in a declining building. Constraints: at least
three details that map class; one comic detail that is also sad; dialogue that evades a direct question; no
solution; end on a threshold.
