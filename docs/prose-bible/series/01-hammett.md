# 01 · Dashiell Hammett — The Continental Op

> **Card** · 1923–1930 · 2 novels + core stories · hardboiled · 1st person / past · San Francisco,
> Personville, New York
> **Read first:** *Red Harvest* (1929)
> **Popularity:** foundational rather than mass-market. *Red Harvest* and *The Maltese Falcon* have never
> been out of print, and Chandler's essay on Hammett is the origin of the genre's self-description.
> **Voice card:** sentence 3 · variance 4 · figuration 2 · interiority 2 · dialogue 7 · description 3 ·
> violence 5 · irony 5 · fatalism 6 · procedure 6 · sex 2 · distance 8

## What the series solved

Hammett invented the modern crime sentence. Before him, detective fiction was mostly a machine for
delivering clues in polite prose. Hammett's Op — fat, middle-aged, unnamed, salaried by a detective agency
— investigates not a puzzle but a **power system**, and the prose records only what can be seen and heard.
The result is a narrator who seems to have no inner life at all, and whose withheld judgment becomes the
most expressive thing on the page.

The Op's method is also the series' philosophy: find leverage, apply pressure, let people betray each
other. In *Red Harvest*, he arrives in a company town and effectively rents himself out to every faction
until the factions destroy one another. The detective does not restore order; he accelerates entropy and
then leaves.

## Syntax and prose mechanics

- **Sentence.** Declarative and unadorned, with an average around thirteen words in the early work
  (William Marling's measurement). Fight scenes drop to eight; descriptive passages are kept under
  fifteen, beyond which, Marling notes, they "run into flab."
- **Paragraph.** Short, built from action + object + speech. There is almost no transitional
  throat-clearing. When a paragraph needs to explain, it does so in a sentence and moves on.
- **Dialogue.** Runs long, carries the plot, and is tagged almost exclusively with "said." Hammett's ear is
  for the way people use speech to negotiate position: who answers too fast, who repeats a question, who
  volunteers a detail.
- **Description.** Functional and evidential. Objects are described because they can be checked: a hat, a
  scar, the way a man holds a cigarette. The Op notices what a detective would need in order to act.
- **Figuration.** Almost none. When a metaphor appears it is short, ugly, and useful. This is the original
  anti-lyrical position in the genre, and it is a deliberate ethical stance: eloquence is a way of getting
  between the reader and the fact.
- **Punctuation.** Periods. Occasional commas. Very few semicolons, dashes, or exclamation marks.
- **Exposition.** Delivered by phone call, file, or somebody talking. Never by the narrator explaining the
  world.

> The Op's own aesthetic is stated in one of the stories: "I don't like eloquence: if it isn't effective
> enough to pierce your hide, it's tiresome; and if it is effective enough, then it muddles your thoughts."

That is the whole style, in one sentence by the character.

## Character psychology

**The Op** is the first protagonist in the genre whose psychology is expressed as *method*. He has no name,
no home, no family, no stated politics. What he has is a professional code: he will lie, bribe, and set
people against each other, but he will not take a personal bribe, and he does not enjoy cruelty. His body
is described with comic realism — thinning hair, thickening belly, back trouble — which keeps him from
becoming a fantasy of power.

**Antagonists** are rarely individual monsters. They are businessmen, police chiefs, union men, and
racketeers whose interests have momentarily aligned. Hammett's insight — inherited by Ellroy and by every conspiracy noir since — is that **corruption is administrative**.
The villain is a system that has learned to sign its name.

**Supporting characters** are defined by appetite and position: the woman who wants out, the cop who wants
a cut, the idealist who wants to be told he is still idealistic. They are not rounded in the modernist
sense; they are *legible*, which is what the Op needs.

## Signature devices

1. **Behaviourist narration.** Report only what can be observed. Withhold the narrator's opinion until an
   action reveals it.
2. **Load-bearing detail.** Every described object should be usable later as evidence, leverage, or
   characterisation. If it does none of those, cut it.
3. **Leverage as plot engine.** Scenes should end with someone obtaining something another person needs.
4. **The flat declarative.** "I put the photograph in my pocket and took the train that night." Two verbs,
   no commentary, entire decision implied.
5. **Withheld judgment.** Let the reader supply the moral reaction the narrator refuses to state.

## What to steal / what to avoid

**Steal:** the discipline of the observable; dialogue as negotiation; the refusal to explain a character
by describing their feelings.

**Avoid:** mistaking flatness for emptiness. Hammett's flatness is *selected* — the details are load-bearing
because he chose them. Imitators cut all interiority and keep all the boring objects.

## Diagnostic passage (original, in the voice)

> A man named Walsh came to the office at nine and said he had a daughter in Poisonville who had stopped
> writing. He had a photograph of her in a paper folder, and he opened it on my desk and turned it so the
> light fell on it. She was twenty-three and smiled at the camera as if it had said something funny.
> Walsh's hands were careful with the folder. He was a small man in a brown suit, and his collar was
> frayed on the left side, where the razor had caught it.
>
> "She writes every Sunday," he said. "She hasn't written since the eleventh."
>
> I put the photograph in my pocket and took the train that night.

**Annotation**

- Nine sentences, roughly twelve words each, with one longer sentence carrying the emotional weight
  (the collar).
- No stated feeling. Walsh's love is in "careful with the folder"; his poverty is in the frayed collar;
  the Op's decision is in two verbs.
- The one simile is domestic and slightly odd ("as if it had said something funny"), which is as much
  figuration as the voice will tolerate.
- The paragraph ends on an action, not a reflection. That is the Hammett ending.

## Exercise

Write 400 words in which a client hires the Op to find a missing adult. Constraints: no sentence over 20
words; no interiority; no adverb modifying a dialogue tag; one object that will matter later; one lie told
by the client that the reader can detect but the Op does not comment on.
