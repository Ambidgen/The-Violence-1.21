# 03 · Character Psychology: the people inside the sentences

> **Content note:** this chapter discusses trauma, addiction, psychopathy, sexual violence, and suicide as
> craft problems. It is not clinical advice. If you are writing about a real disorder, read the clinical
> literature and, where possible, talk to people with lived experience.

## 1. Why psychology is plot

A crime novel is a machine for revealing people under pressure. The plot supplies the pressure; the
psychology supplies the machine's shape. When the two are not connected, the book feels like a case
report with a personality taped to it.

The canon's most durable characters can be described by five elements. This is not a screenwriting formula;
it is a description of how the books actually work.

| Element | Question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wound** | What happened that cannot be undone? | Bosch's mother's murder; Robicheaux's war; Wallander's solitude |
| **Lie** | What do they believe about themselves that is not true? | Rebus believes he is the only one who can do the job; Scarpetta believes care can prevent death |
| **Want** | What are they chasing in the present? | The case, the truth, the next drink, the next fix |
| **Need** | What would actually help them? | Connection, forgiveness, rest, surrender |
| **Cost** | What does their way of coping cost other people? | Relationships, careers, children, colleagues |

The plot should test the lie. The ending should either break it or prove it — and both are valid. What is
not valid is a plot that never touches it.

## 2. Eight protagonist engines

The canon repeats eight psychological designs. Most characters are a blend of two.

### 2.1 The code hero

**Wound:** the world is corrupt; the hero is not.
**Lie:** "If I keep the code, I will stay clean."
**Want:** justice for someone who cannot get it.
**Cost:** loneliness; the code becomes a way of refusing intimacy.

**Examples:** Marlowe, Archer, Bosch, V.I.

The code hero works when the code is **tested and expensive**. It fails when the code is a superpower that
makes the hero morally superior to everyone else. Chandler's Marlowe is beaten and humiliated; Paretsky's
V.I. is injured and frightened; Connelly's Bosch loses jobs and relationships. The code costs.

### 2.2 The wounded healer

**Wound:** a specific, named trauma.
**Lie:** "If I save others, I do not have to save myself."
**Want:** repair, usually for someone else.
**Cost:** self-neglect, relapse, vicarious trauma.

**Examples:** Robicheaux, Wallander, Harry Hole, Will Graham.

The wounded healer is the canon's dominant model, and the most easily sentimentalised. The cure is
**specificity**: not "he had a dark past," but a date, a name, a smell, a recurring dream, a physical
scar, a behaviour that recurs under stress.

### 2.3 The institutionalist

**Wound:** the job has taken more than it has given.
**Lie:** "The institution is worth it, or will be, if I keep showing up."
**Want:** to do the work well.
**Cost:** family, health, faith.

**Examples:** Martin Beck, Steve Carella, Wallander, Scarpetta.

This engine is about **endurance rather than damage**. The drama is bureaucratic and moral: budgets,
politics, colleagues who cut corners, a public that does not care. The psychological payoff is the moment
the institutionalist admits what the job has cost — usually to themselves, usually in a car.

### 2.4 The divided cop

**Wound:** complicity.
**Lie:** "I can manage the compromise."
**Want:** to keep the job and the self.
**Cost:** the compromise always collects.

**Examples:** Rebus, Ellroy's ensembles, Himes's Grave Digger and Coffin Ed.

The divided cop is the canon's richest ethical figure because the conflict is internal and structural at
once. The character is loyal to a community, an institution, and a partner, and those loyalties cannot all
be honoured. The best scenes are the ones where the character chooses, and the choice is wrong in a way
they can see.

### 2.5 The obsessive

**Wound:** a case that was not closed.
**Lie:** "If I solve this one, I will be free."
**Want:** the answer.
**Cost:** everything else.

**Examples:** Bosch, Rebus, Harry Hole, Scarpetta at her worst.

Obsession is a plot engine disguised as a personality. It generates forward motion and moral blindness at
the same time. To make it credible, show the **opportunity cost** in concrete scenes: the missed dinner,
the lie to a colleague, the file taken home, the sleep lost.

### 2.6 The competent stranger

**Wound:** usually withheld.
**Lie:** "I need nothing."
**Want:** to fix the immediate problem.
**Cost:** none to him; the cost is displaced onto the people he protects.

**Examples:** Reacher; the Op in some stories; the classic western drifter.

This is the canon's fantasy engine, and it works only if the book is honest about the fantasy. Reacher's
freedom is pleasurable because it is impossible: no possessions, no dependents, no consequences that
cannot be walked away from. A literary novel that pretended this was realism would fail; a thriller that
delivers it cleanly succeeds.

### 2.7 The professional witness

**Wound:** knowledge.
**Lie:** "Objectivity will protect me."
**Want:** to record accurately.
**Cost:** numbness; the inability to act.

**Examples:** Scarpetta, Clarice Starling, Blomkvist (a journalist variant).

The witness's psychology is organised around **attention**. Their competence is observation; their danger
is that observation becomes a substitute for action, or that the thing observed enters them. Cornwell's
autopsy scenes and Harris's interviews both dramatise this.

### 2.8 The outlaw

**Wound:** the law has already failed them.
**Lie:** "I am outside it now."
**Want:** survival, revenge, or a score.
**Cost:** the people who still believe in them.

**Examples:** Leonard's professionals, Himes's adversaries, the *romans durs* protagonists.

Noir proper is built on this engine. The outlaw is not a detective; the story is about the consequences of
a choice rather than the solution to a crime. The psychology is fatalistic: the character's own appetite
is the antagonist.

## 3. Trauma, rendered accurately

### 3.1 The four symptom clusters

Post-traumatic stress disorder is diagnosed across four clusters. For fiction, the useful question is not
"does the character have PTSD?" but **which cluster does the scene show?**

| Cluster | On the page | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intrusion** | Flashbacks, nightmares, sensory triggers, sudden re-experiencing | Cinematic flashback with full explanation |
| **Avoidance** | Dodging places, people, conversations; workaholism; emotional numbing | Generic "he didn't want to talk about it" |
| **Hyperarousal** | Startle response, irritability, sleep loss, scanning exits | Constant rage; no context |
| **Negative cognition/mood** | Shame, guilt, "I am damaged," memory gaps, detachment | Self-pity without behaviour |

A character can have one cluster and not the others. A veteran who is hypervigilant but not avoidant is a
different person from one who is numb and avoidant. Choose.

### 3.2 Moral injury

Moral injury is the distress that follows **perpetrating, failing to prevent, bearing witness to, or
learning about acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs** (Litz et al., 2009). It is not the same as
fear-based trauma. Its signature emotions are **guilt, shame, betrayal, disgust, and anger**, and its
consequence is often withdrawal and a loss of trust.

For crime fiction this is the most important construct in the chapter, because police work is full of
morally injurious events: a decision that gets someone killed, a colleague's cover-up, a system that
protects the wrong person, a victim who is not saved.

Moral injury is rendered through:

- **self-condemnation that is specific** ("I chose the door on the left"),
- **betrayal by an institution** (the department, the bureau, the church),
- **rituals of expiation** (work, drink, confession, punishment),
- **the inability to accept forgiveness**.

> Bosch's "Everybody counts or nobody counts" is a moral-injury management strategy: an absolute rule
> adopted to survive a world that keeps proving it false.

### 3.3 Complex trauma and dissociation

Complex trauma arises from prolonged, repeated exposure — childhood abuse, domestic captivity, institutional
care. Its features include emotional dysregulation, negative self-concept, dissociation, and difficulty
with relationships. Dissociation can range from mild spacing-out to depersonalisation and memory gaps.

**Salander** is the canon's most fully realised complex-trauma survivor: she is not a collection of
symptoms, but her behaviour — silence, strategic opacity, difficulty with touch, absolute moral
boundaries — is consistent with a history of institutional and familial abuse. Crucially, she is also
**competent, angry, and funny**, which is what prevents her from becoming a case study.

**Writing rule:** trauma explains, but it does not exhaust. A survivor is also a person with preferences,
jokes, skills, and a life. If every scene is a symptom, the character has become a diagnosis.

### 3.4 Betrayal trauma

When the harm is done by someone the person depends on — a parent, a spouse, an institution — the
psychological response often includes **idealisation of the abuser, self-blame, and memory fragmentation**.
This is the engine of many family-secret plots (Macdonald, Larsson, Rankin). The detective's job is to
make the unspoken speakable, which is why the interview in these books so often feels like therapy.

### 3.5 Accuracy tests for a trauma scene

1. **Is the trigger specific and sensory?** (A smell, a sound, a phrase — not "he remembered the war.")
2. **Does the reaction cost something in the scene?** (He drops the cup, loses the interview, snaps at the
   wrong person.)
3. **Is there a before and an after?** (Trauma is not a single crescendo; it is a pattern.)
4. **Does the character have a coping strategy that is not just "being sad"?** (Work, drink, ritual,
   avoidance, humor, control.)
5. **Does another character have to deal with it?** (Trauma is relational; isolation is a symptom, not a
   setting.)

If a scene fails three of these, it is probably using trauma as atmosphere.

## 4. Addiction

Addiction is the canon's second great psychology, and it is usually handled badly. The bad version: a
flask, a hangover, a quip. The good version: a **cycle** with costs, rituals, and consequences.

### 4.1 The cycle

- **Trigger:** stress, shame, celebration, boredom, physical pain.
- **Ritual:** the specific behaviour — the brand, the route to the bar, the hiding place, the time of day.
- **Relief:** brief, real, and therefore reinforcing.
- **Cost:** memory loss, lie, injury, relationship damage, professional risk.
- **Resolve:** promises, rules, bargaining ("only on weekends").
- **Relapse:** often triggered by the shame of the resolve failing.

The canon's best addiction writing is **specific about ritual**:

- Burke's Robicheaux is alert to the exact moment a drink becomes possible; sobriety is a daily practice,
  not a cured state.
- Nesbø's Harry Hole has blackouts, physical withdrawal, and the humiliation of being found; the addiction
  is a plot mechanism, not a mood.
- Rankin's Rebus uses alcohol as a social tool and a self-medication, and the books show the slow
  accumulation of damage rather than a single dramatic collapse.
- Wallander's drinking is ordinary, embarrassed, and tied to loneliness; it is not glamorous.

### 4.2 Self-medication

The self-medication hypothesis (Khantzian, 1997) holds that people use substances to manage unbearable
affect — anxiety, rage, shame, dissociation. For fiction, this means the substance should be **doing a job**
for the character, and the job should be visible. A detective who drinks to sleep is a different person
from one who drinks to feel brave, and a different person again from one who drinks to stop remembering.

### 4.3 Accuracy tests

1. Is the ritual specific?
2. Is the relief real?
3. Is the cost paid by someone other than the addict?
4. Does the character lie about it in a way that is characteristic?
5. Is sobriety portrayed as effortful rather than triumphant?

## 5. Shame, guilt, and the Catholic imagination

Guilt says *I did something bad*. Shame says *I am bad*. The distinction matters because the canon's
protagonists are usually organised around one or the other.

- **Guilt** produces confession, reparation, and narrative resolution. It is the engine of Macdonald's
  family plots and Rankin's institutional reckonings.
- **Shame** produces concealment, rage, and self-destruction. It is the engine of Ellroy's protagonists and
  Nesbø's Hole.

Burke's Catholicism is the canon's most explicit treatment: sin, confession, penance, and grace are
structural, not decorative. The novels are honest that the framework can be a refuge as well as a
discipline, and that grace is not earned.

**Writing rule:** do not let a character announce "I felt ashamed." Show the behaviour shame produces:
the inability to meet an eye, the pre-emptive lie, the rage at being seen, the compulsive reparation.

## 6. Antagonist psychology

### 6.1 Four modes

| Mode | Logic | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Predator** | Appetite, ritual, fantasy | Harris, Nesbø, Cornwell's later books |
| **System** | Profit, reputation, institutional survival | Ellroy, Sjöwall & Wahlöö, Paretsky, Larsson |
| **Mirror** | The detective's own shadow | Rankin's Cafferty, Himes's cops, Ellroy |
| **Ordinary person** | Fear, jealousy, shame, one bad decision | Simenon, Macdonald, Grafton, Mankell |

The canon's most frightening villains are usually a blend: a predator protected by a system, or an ordinary
person whose shame has become a system.

### 6.2 Psychopathy without the Hollywood version

Clinical and research models describe psychopathy along dimensions, not as a single monster. The
**triarchic model** (Patrick, Fowles & Krueger, 2009) distinguishes:

- **Boldness** — low fear, stress tolerance, dominance, social confidence.
- **Meanness** — callousness, lack of empathy, exploitativeness, cruelty.
- **Disinhibition** — impulsivity, poor planning, hostility, weak restraint.

The PCL-R assesses interpersonal, affective, lifestyle, and antisocial facets. Not every person with high
psychopathic traits is violent; many are not. Not every violent person is psychopathic. The diagnosis is
not a synonym for "evil."

**Writing the deficits, not the label:**

- **Affect**: shallow, short-lived, and poorly matched to content. The smile arrives late or not at all.
- **Fear**: notably absent where it should be present. This is more unsettling than rage.
- **Empathy**: not the absence of all feeling, but the inability to use another person's feeling as a
  reason. The character can *read* people — often very well — without being moved by them.
- **Deception**: conning and manipulative, often charming, often unnecessary. The lie is a habit.
- **Lifestyle**: parasitic, impulsive, irresponsible; promises without future tense.
- **Grandiosity**: a self-story that is internally coherent and externally absurd.

**Lecter** is the canon's great aestheticised version: boldness plus meanness, with disinhibition largely
absent. He is controlled, courteous, and vain. **Himes's** killers are often disinhibited and grotesque.
**Mankell's** killers are often sad and ordinary. The contrast is a useful reminder that "the mind of a
killer" is not one mind.

### 6.3 The banality problem

Arendt's phrase is often misused, but the underlying observation is useful for crime fiction: the most
dangerous people are often not monsters but **administrators**. Sjöwall & Wahlöö, Ellroy, Paretsky, and
Larsson all build villains whose evil is procedural — a signature, a budget line, a decision to look away.
The psychology of such a villain is not appetite but **justification**. Show the story they tell
themselves; that is where the horror is.

### 6.4 The sympathetic culprit

Macdonald's innovation, inherited by Rankin and others: the culprit is often a person repeating an injury.
The reveal should produce pity as well as justice. The risk is excusing; the cure is to keep the
consequences visible — the victim is still dead, the family is still ruined.

## 7. The profiler and the cost of empathy

Will Graham and Clarice Starling are the canon's two great studies in **empathy as contamination**.

- Graham can think like a killer, and the thinking damages him. His gift is also a symptom.
- Starling is ambitious and ashamed, and Lecter trades information for her memories. The relationship is
  exploitative, and she is an agent in it.

**Rules for writing a profiler:**

1. **No magic.** Deduction must be grounded in evidence the reader has seen.
2. **Cost.** Every descent into a killer's mind should cost the profiler something — sleep, appetite,
   sexual function, a relationship, a belief.
3. **Contamination.** The profiler should recognise something of themselves in the killer, and be changed
   by the recognition.
4. **Institutional use.** The bureau should be using the profiler, not merely employing them. The ethics of
   that use are part of the story.

## 8. Victims, families, and the ethics of the body

### 8.1 The victim as a person

The canon's most ethical books give the victim **a life before the crime**: a job, a habit, a relationship, a
contradiction. Connelly's cold cases are built on this; Macdonald's plots are elegiac because the dead are
still present in the family system; Larsson's victims are tied to an argument about power.

**The fridging test:** if the victim's only function is to give the protagonist a motive, the book is using
a person as a prop. Give the victim at least one scene, memory, or detail that is not about the
protagonist.

### 8.2 The family as a psychological system

Macdonald, Larsson, Rankin, and Burke all use the family as the deep structure of crime:

- **The secret** (Macdonald): a past event that everyone knows and no one says.
- **The scapegoat** (Macdonald): the child who carries the family's shame.
- **The inheritance** (Burke, Larsson): money, violence, and reputation passed down.
- **The absent parent** (Rankin, Connelly): the wound that organises the detective's work.

Family systems are useful because they make psychology **structural**: the character is not just damaged;
they are *positioned*. A daughter who was the caretaker, a son who was the golden child, a mother who was
the family's PR department — these roles generate behaviour without requiring a flashback.

### 8.3 Aftermath

The canon's best books show the aftermath of violence: the hospital, the funeral, the press conference, the
empty flat, the colleague who cannot stop washing his hands. Aftermath is where the prose earns its
seriousness.

## 9. Gender and sexuality

The canon's gender politics are uneven, and pretending otherwise would make this bible useless.

- **The femme fatale** is a real archetype and a real problem: she is often intelligent and trapped, but she
  is also frequently a projection of the male narrator's fear. A modern version needs her own want, her own
  scene, and a consequence that is not just the hero's disillusionment.
- **The feminist PI** (Paretsky, Grafton) changed the genre by making the detective a working woman with a
  community, a body, and a politics. Paretsky has said she deliberately refused to use sex to exploit her
  characters or readers; V.I. is sexual and moral at once.
- **The male gaze** is a craft issue, not a moralistic one: it determines what the prose notices. If every
  woman is described by her body and every man by his job, the narration has a politics whether the author
  intends it or not.
- **Writing across gender** requires the same discipline as writing across class or race: specificity,
  agency, and a refusal to make the character a symbol of their category.

## 10. Institutional psychology

Crime fiction is one of the few popular forms that can show how organisations think.

- **The police** are a hierarchy of discretion: who gets investigated, who gets believed, who gets the
  budget. Rankin, Connelly, and Sjöwall & Wahlöö are the canon's best sociologists of this.
- **The forensic lab** is a priesthood with its own politics (Cornwell).
- **The press** is a parallel investigation with different incentives (Larsson, Rankin).
- **The bureau** is a machine for converting empathy into evidence (Harris).

**Moral injury is usually organisational**: the character is not traumatised by a single event but by
watching an institution choose reputation over truth. Show the meeting, the memo, the press line. That is
where the psychology lives.

## 11. Rendering techniques

Psychology is not what a character says about themselves. It is what the prose notices.

### 11.1 The tell

Give each major character one **involuntary behaviour** that appears under stress: a hand moved to a pocket,
a rehearsed smile, a repeated phrase, a way of sitting. Introduce it in a low-stakes scene; pay it off in a
high-stakes one.

### 11.2 The object

People are revealed by what they keep, hide, repair, or throw away. A frayed collar, a photograph in a
folder, a melted earring, a boat with a date on the back. The object should be **specific** and **usable**.

### 11.3 The lie

Every character should lie about something, and the lie should be **characteristic**: the way they lie tells
you who they are. A vain man lies to look competent; a shamed woman lies to protect someone else; a
psychopath lies when the truth would work just as well.

### 11.4 What they notice

Point of view is a selection device. A cop notices exits; a forensic pathologist notices the body; a
journalist notices the official story; a criminal notices the camera. If two characters would notice
different things in the same room, you have two points of view.

### 11.5 Free indirect thought

Use the character's vocabulary in the narration without quotation marks. This is the most efficient way to
create intimacy while keeping third person. See [`02-prose-and-syntax.md`](02-prose-and-syntax.md#65-free-indirect-style).

### 11.6 Silence

The canon's most powerful psychological moments are often silent: a character not answering, a witness
leaving, a detective sitting in a car. Do not fill the silence with explanation.

## 12. Character dossier template

Copy this for every major character. Keep it under two pages.

```
NAME / AGE / JOB
PUBLIC STORY:            what they tell strangers
PRIVATE STORY:           what they tell no one
WOUND:                   specific event, date, sensory detail
LIE:                     the belief that protects the wound
WANT (external):         the case-level goal
NEED (internal):         what would actually help
COST:                    who pays for their coping
COPING:                  ritual, substance, work, humor, control
TELL:                    involuntary behaviour under stress
OBJECT:                  what they keep or hide
LIE IN SPEECH:           how they lie, not just what about
RELATIONSHIP TO POWER:   rank, class, race, gender, institution
CHANGE:                  what breaks, what holds
```

If you cannot fill in WOUND, LIE, and COST, the character is not yet a character; they are a function.

## 13. Clichés to retire

- The alcoholic detective who is charming about it.
- The dead wife whose only function is motivation.
- The serial killer who is a genius aesthete and quotes philosophy.
- The therapist who explains the protagonist to the reader.
- The rookie who exists to be shocked.
- The corrupt cop who is corrupt for no reason.
- The victim who is beautiful, young, and described only as a body.
- The interrogation where the suspect confesses because the detective is tough.
- The trauma flashback that arrives exactly when the plot needs a pause.
- The diagnosis used as a personality ("she was borderline"; "he was a psychopath").

## 14. Accuracy checklist

Before you sign off a psychologically loaded scene:

- [ ] Is the symptom specific to a cluster, not a generic mood?
- [ ] Does the scene show a **trigger → reaction → cost** sequence?
- [ ] Does the character have a coping strategy that predates the scene?
- [ ] Is another person affected by the behaviour?
- [ ] Is the clinical vocabulary used only by a character who would use it?
- [ ] Does the character have a life beyond the diagnosis?
- [ ] Is the villain's psychology explained by **behaviour**, not by a lecture?
- [ ] Is the victim a person before and after the crime?
- [ ] Is the violence/sex filtered through a point of view with a stake in it?
- [ ] Does the scene avoid using trauma as a plot convenience?
