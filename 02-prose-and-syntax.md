# 02 · Prose and Syntax: what the canon actually does on the page

This chapter is the comparative anatomy. It treats the twenty series as a single body of evidence and asks
the same questions of each: how long are the sentences, how do they vary, what holds a paragraph together,
who speaks and how, how much interiority is licensed, and where does the plot get its information.

The series cards give the local detail. This chapter gives the laws.

---

## 1. Five registers, and one hybrid

Every series in the canon runs on one of five prose registers, sometimes with a second in a supporting
role. The register determines what the prose is *for*.

| Register | What it does | Primary series | Signature risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Objective** | Reports behaviour; withholds interiority | Hammett, Leonard, Child | Thinness; characters as machines |
| **Lyric** | Uses image and rhythm to judge the world | Chandler, Macdonald, Burke | Overripeness; beauty that cushions horror |
| **Telegraphic** | Compresses by removing connective tissue | Ellroy, late Child | Obscurity; parody |
| **Dialogic** | Advances through speech and subtext | Leonard, McBain, Rankin | Cryptic talk; talking heads |
| **Procedural** | Creates suspense through ordered work | Connelly, Cornwell, Mankell, Sjöwall & Wahlöö | Manualitis; procedure without cost |
| **Gothic** (hybrid) | Procedural exactness + lurid image | Harris, Himes, Nesbø | Aestheticised cruelty |

Two conclusions follow immediately.

First, **register is an ethical choice, not a decorative one.** Hammett's flatness refuses to let the
narrator's sensibility sit between the reader and the fact. Burke's lyricism insists that beauty is a form
of moral evidence. Neither is more "literary"; they are different claims about what a crime novel is for.

Second, **the most imitated writers are the ones with the clearest permissions.** Chandler permits himself
similes and forbids himself plot tidiness. Leonard forbids adverbs and permits coincidence of character.
Ellroy permits fragments and forbids transitions. A voice is a set of permissions and prohibitions, and
the prohibitions do most of the work.

## 2. The sentence

### 2.1 Length is not style; variance is

A page of uniformly medium sentences reads as dead no matter what the average is. The canon's most
distinctive voices are distinguished by **variance**, not length.

- **Hammett**: low mean (~13 words in the early work, per William Marling's measurement), low variance.
  The effect is a steady, unblinking camera. Fights drop to ~8 words; Marling notes that descriptive
  passages "run into flab" at fifteen words.
- **Chandler**: medium mean, **high variance**. A 35-word sentence of atmospheric clauses followed by a
  5-word verdict. The rhythm is comic: setup, setup, punchline.
- **Ellroy**: very low mean, low variance, but the *paragraph* has high variance — a sequence of 3-word
  fragments can end in a 20-word alliterative detonation.
- **Burke**: high mean, high variance. A 60-word sentence of accumulation followed by a 4-word refusal.
- **Child**: low mean, controlled variance. Fragments and one-clause sentences, with occasional longer
  sentences used as summary.

**Rule:** decide your voice's *variance*, then write to it. If every sentence is 12–18 words, you do not
have a style; you have a default.

### 2.2 Parataxis and hypotaxis

**Parataxis** — clauses joined by *and*, *then*, *so*, with little subordination — creates speed,
flatness, and a sense that events are simply happening. It is the grammar of Hammett, Leonard, Child, and
much of Ellroy.

> "He put the photograph in my pocket and took the train that night." (Hammett pattern)

**Hypotaxis** — clauses nested with *because*, *although*, *which*, *as if* — creates analysis, irony, and
a narrator who is judging while reporting. It is the grammar of Chandler, Macdonald, Burke, and Rankin.

> "She walked in without knocking, which is the kind of thing that either means money or means trouble."
> (Chandler pattern)

Most pastiche fails because it copies vocabulary while keeping the wrong grammar. A Chandler sentence with
parataxis is just a private-eye cliché; a Hammett sentence with hypotaxis is a lawyer's memo.

### 2.3 The four sentence engines

The canon uses four repeatable sentence shapes. Learn them as units, not as abstractions.

1. **The flat declarative.** Subject + verb + object, no modifiers.
   *"The lab boys were still working the far end of the alley."*
   Use for: action, refusal of emotion, establishing fact.

2. **The cumulative sentence.** Main clause first, then participles and *and* clauses piling up sensory
   detail. Burke's signature; also Chandler's descriptive mode.
   *"The rain had stopped by morning, and the bayou was the colour of old copper, and the cypress roots
   stood in the water like the ribs of something that had died…"*
   Use for: landscape, memory, the pressure of atmosphere.

3. **The periodic sentence.** Subordinate clauses first, main clause delayed to the end. Chandler uses it
   for punchlines; Ellroy uses it rarely and violently.
   *"If you want a three-dimensional character, you need chunks of prose."* (Rankin's own formulation)
   Use for: judgment, reversal, a closing snap.

4. **The fragment.** Verbless or subjectless. A pressure device, not a default.
   *"Two cars. No wallet. No keys."*
   Use for: speed, shock, list-as-evidence.

### 2.4 Sound

The canon is full of sound-patterning, and the patterning is functional.

- **Alliteration** binds fragments in Ellroy and gives Himes's comic set pieces their chant.
- **Assonance** softens Chandler's endings; the long vowels slow the reader at the moment of judgment.
- **Anaphora** (repetition at the start of clauses) creates urgency in Nesbø and Harris.
- **Epistrophe** (repetition at the end) creates closure and menace: "Nothing happened to me. I happened."
- **Polysyndeton** (many *and*s) creates the tidal effect in Burke and the childlike inevitability in
  McCarthy-adjacent crime prose.
- **Asyndeton** (no conjunctions) creates the staccato of Ellroy and the report-style of Connelly.

**Test:** read the paragraph aloud. If the stresses fall in the same place every sentence, the rhythm is
wrong even if the grammar is correct.

### 2.5 Punctuation as character

| Mark | Effect in the canon | Overuse symptom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Period | Authority, refusal, camera-stop | Monotony |
| Comma | Flow, qualification, breath | Breathlessness, comma splice |
| Semicolon | Analytic distance; Chandler, Rankin, Burke | Literary fussiness in a hardboiled voice |
| Em dash | Interruption, self-correction, speed | Melodrama; the "TV recap" dash |
| Ellipsis | Trailing off, defeat | Vagueness; the amateur's suspense |
| Exclamation | Comic shock; Himes, McBain | Loss of authority |
| Italics | Intrusive thought; Harris's free indirect | Soap opera |

Leonard's rule stands: no more than two or three exclamation marks per 100,000 words. Ellroy's slashes and
dashes are not decoration; they are the notation for a compressed breath.

## 3. The paragraph

### 3.1 Chandler's half-sheet

Chandler wrote on half-sheets of about 125–150 words and required each one to contain a detail, a
description, or an action that advanced the scene. This is the most useful paragraph rule in the canon:
**every unit of the page must contain something the scene needs.**

A practical version for modern manuscripts: **every 120–180 words, something must change** — a fact is
learned, a position shifts, a physical object enters, a decision is made. If a paragraph contains only
atmosphere or only reflection, it is a candidate for cutting or merging.

### 3.2 The five paragraph shapes

1. **Action block** (Hammett, Child, Connelly): short sentences, physical verbs, no interiority. Ends on a
   change of position.
2. **Tableau** (Chandler, Burke, Ellroy): a still image loaded with meaning; the paragraph is a painting
   with a judgment in it.
3. **Interview** (Macdonald, Rankin, Paretsky, Harris): dialogue plus action beats; information and
   evasion alternate.
4. **Montage** (Ellroy, Larsson, Nesbø): short scenes cut together by date, place, or image; the meaning is
   in the juxtaposition.
5. **Interior block** (Rankin, Burke, Mankell, Cornwell): the narrator thinking; must still advance the
   case or the character, or it is an essay.

### 3.3 Opening and closing the paragraph

The canon's paragraphs tend to open on a **fact** and close on a **judgment or image**. The fact grounds the
reader; the close gives the paragraph its charge.

- Chandler: fact → observation → joke that judges.
- Hammett: fact → action → action.
- Macdonald: place → object → elegy.
- Ellroy: fragment → fragment → grotesque image.
- Leonard: action → dialogue → deflation.

If your paragraphs open on reflection and close on fact, the voice will feel inverted and limp.

## 4. Dialogue

Dialogue is where the canon is most teachable, because the rules are concrete.

### 4.1 The tag discipline

- **"Said" is invisible; everything else is audible.** Leonard's rule — never a verb other than "said" —
  is a default, not a law. The exceptions in the canon are usually comic (McBain) or formal (Harris).
- **No adverbs on "said."** If the line needs an adverb, the line is wrong. Rewrite the speech.
- **Action beats replace tags.** A character lighting a cigarette, refusing a chair, or checking a phone
  identifies the speaker and characterises them at once.
- **Untagged is fine when the idiolects are distinct.** McBain and Leonard can run eight lines without a
  tag because each voice has a different rhythm and vocabulary.

### 4.2 Subtext

The canon's best dialogue is about the thing *under* the thing.

- In **Leonard**, characters negotiate a scam while discussing something trivial.
- In **Chandler**, characters flirt and threaten in the same sentence.
- In **Harris**, courtesy is the threat.
- In **Rankin**, insult is affection.
- In **Paretsky**, a bureaucratic exchange is a class war.

**Rule:** every scene of dialogue should have a **transaction** (what each person wants from the other) and
an **obstacle** (why they will not simply say it).

### 4.3 Idiolect

Give each major character:

- a **sentence length** (short/long),
- a **habit** (repeats the other person's name, answers questions with questions, uses technical jargon),
- a **lie** they tell in speech (a verbal tic that reveals what they are hiding),
- a **register** (formal, slangy, liturgical, bureaucratic).

Hammett's people are distinguished by what they volunteer; Leonard's by what they avoid; McBain's by
professional shorthand; Harris's by etiquette.

### 4.4 The unanswerable question

The canon's most tense conversations often contain a question that is not answered. The absence is the
information. Do not fill it with a narrator's explanation; let the next action carry the meaning.

## 5. Description and figuration

### 5.1 The simile budget

Chandler is the high-water mark, and even he is disciplined. A working budget for a crime novel:

- **Lyric register:** 2–4 similes per 1,000 words, each doing diagnostic work.
- **Objective/dialogic register:** 0–1 per 1,000 words.
- **Gothic register:** 3–5 per 1,000 words, but the images must escalate in kind, not just in number.

The test is not "is it original?" but **"what does it tell me that a plain statement would not?"** If the
answer is "the writer is clever," cut it.

### 5.2 Source domains

The canon draws images from a small number of domains, and the domain is a worldview:

| Domain | Series | What it implies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domestic/kitchen | Chandler, Simenon | The extraordinary hidden in the ordinary |
| Mechanical/industrial | Chandler, Paretsky, Connelly | The city as machine |
| Natural/weather | Macdonald, Burke, Mankell | The human as part of a landscape |
| Religious/architectural | Burke, Rankin, Harris | Guilt, hierarchy, the sacred |
| Commercial/financial | Paretsky, Larsson | Power as a transaction |
| Bodily/medical | Cornwell, Harris | The body as evidence and site of horror |

If your images all come from the same domain, your narrator has only one way of seeing. If they come from
none, the narrator has no sensibility.

### 5.3 Description of place

The canon's places are **arguments about power**:

- Chandler's Los Angeles: money pretending to be taste.
- Macdonald's California: the family secret under the development.
- Rankin's Edinburgh: vertical class map, "fur coat and no knickers."
- Connelly's Los Angeles: a grid of jurisdictions and forgotten files.
- Mankell's Skåne: silence and distance.
- Paretsky's Chicago: neighbourhoods as political constituencies.

**Rule:** describe a place by who can afford to be there, who is excluded, and what the architecture is
hiding. Weather is only useful if it changes what people can do.

### 5.4 Description of people

Leonard's rule — avoid detailed descriptions of characters — is a useful default, but the canon's best
character descriptions are **metonymic**: one object or physical habit stands for the whole person.

- Chandler: a woman's smile "like a theater curtain."
- Hammett: a frayed collar where the razor caught it.
- Ellroy: a melting earring under a hot radiator.
- Simenon: a man who keeps looking at his watch although no tram has passed.

**Rule:** one detail, chosen for what it implies. Three adjectives are usually two too many.

## 6. Interiority, point of view, tense

### 6.1 The interiority ladder

Not all first person is intimate, and not all third is distant. The canon uses a ladder:

| Level | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Camera: no access to thought | Hammett's Op at his most withheld |
| 1 | Behaviour and inference only | Leonard, Child |
| 2 | Reported thought, tagged | Early Harris ("*Not totally true*, she thought") |
| 3 | Free indirect thought (unmarked) | Harris, Rankin, Cornwell |
| 4 | Confessional first person | Burke, Grafton, Paretsky |
| 5 | Unreliable/self-deceiving narration | Ellroy's *White Jazz*; Nesbø's addict passages |

**Rule:** pick a level and make it mean something. A detective who never thinks about himself is a
statement about professionalism; a detective who thinks about nothing else is a statement about damage.
Do not drift between levels without a structural reason.

### 6.2 First person

Strengths: intimacy, voice, the pleasure of a narrator with opinions.
Risks: the reader knows only what the narrator knows; the narrator must be present for every scene; the
voice can become a performance that flattens other characters.

The canon's solutions:

- **Hammett** makes the narrator a lens, not a personality.
- **Chandler** makes the narrator a sensibility, and the plot is secondary to the voice.
- **Macdonald** makes the narrator a listener, so other people's stories can occupy the page.
- **Burke** makes the narrator a confessional consciousness, so memory can carry exposition.
- **Grafton/Paretsky** make the narrator a professional explainer, so research can be dramatised.

### 6.3 Third person

- **Limited** (Connelly, Mankell, Rankin, Cornwell's later books): one consciousness at a time; the reader
  shares the detective's ignorance. The default for procedural suspense.
- **Rotating close third** (Ellroy, Nesbø, Larsson, McBain): multiple consciousnesses, each with its own
  access. The risk is a loss of intimacy; the reward is dramatic irony.
- **Omniscient** (rare in this canon): Harris uses a limited omniscience that can enter the killer's
  viewpoint briefly. The effect is dread, not authority.

### 6.4 Tense

The canon is overwhelmingly past tense. Present tense appears in Cornwell from *The Last Precinct* (2000)
and in some later crime fiction; it creates immediacy and a sense of ongoing emergency, but it also makes
retrospection and explanation awkward.

**Rule:** past tense is the default because detection is inherently retrospective. Present tense is a
choice that should be justified by a specific urgency — a chase, a countdown, a narrator who does not yet
know how the story ends.

### 6.5 Free indirect style

The most efficient intimacy device in the canon. Instead of:

> She wondered whether he was lying. He seemed nervous.

write:

> He was nervous. *He's lying*, she thought, and then: *or he's frightened of something else.*

Or, more advanced, drop the tag entirely:

> He was nervous. Lying, probably. Or frightened of something else.

The narration takes on the character's vocabulary and syntax without quotation marks. Harris uses this to
move from procedural report to intimacy; Rankin uses it to move between Rebus's associative mind and the
narrator's cooler judgment.

**Warning:** free indirect style requires a clear "host" consciousness. If the reader cannot tell whose
thought it is, the technique becomes muddle.

## 7. Exposition

Crime fiction has more exposition than literary fiction because it has to explain systems. The canon uses
six engines.

1. **The document** (Larsson, Connelly, Cornwell): a report, file, or record read on the page. The reader
   learns the system and the plot at once.
2. **The interview** (Macdonald, Rankin, Paretsky): a character explains something under pressure, so the
   explanation is also a character beat.
3. **The expert** (Cornwell, Harris, McBain): a professional explains a procedure to a colleague or a
   superior; the explanation creates authority and tension.
4. **The memory** (Burke, Mankell, Rankin): a narrator recalls a relevant past; the exposition is
   characterised by nostalgia or shame.
5. **The montage** (Ellroy, Larsson, Nesbø): headlines, dates, and short scenes carry information without a
   lecturer.
6. **The discovery** (all): the reader learns the rule at the moment it is broken. This is the most
   efficient and the most underused.

**Rule:** exposition should arrive when a character *needs* it, not when the author does. If a character
explains something they would not explain to that person in that room, cut it or move it.

## 8. Chapter and scene architecture

### 8.1 Length

| Pattern | Series | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long chapters, multiple scenes | Rankin, Burke, Larsson | Immersion; the reader lives inside a case |
| Medium chapters, one scene | Connelly, Mankell, McBain | Procedural rhythm; a scene per unit of work |
| Very short chapters | Child, Patterson (outside canon) | Propulsion; the page-turn is structural |
| Fixed architecture | Sjöwall & Wahlöö: 30 chapters × 10 books | A single 300-chapter work; the chapter is a day/step |

### 8.2 Endings of chapters

The canon uses four chapter-end moves:

1. **The discovery** (a fact that re-frames the case).
2. **The threat** (a person or force turns toward the protagonist).
3. **The cut** (a scene ends mid-action; the next chapter moves elsewhere).
4. **The image** (a lyrical or grotesque tableau that holds the emotion).

The weakest is the rhetorical question. The strongest is the discovery that is also a threat.

### 8.3 Scene types

Every crime novel is built from a small repertoire:

- **Intake**: the case arrives; the detective's world is established.
- **Interview**: information under resistance.
- **Discovery**: a document, body, or scene changes the questions.
- **Reversal**: the detective's theory is wrong, or the ally is compromised.
- **Confrontation**: the antagonist and detective meet; power is tested.
- **Aftermath**: the cost is registered; the next step is chosen.
- **Breather**: domestic or comic scene that makes the danger matter.

**Rule:** a scene that does not change the question, the detective's position, or the reader's
understanding is a candidate for cutting.

## 9. Violence and sex

The canon is adult fiction, and the handling of bodies is part of the prose, not a separate department.
The comparative record is instructive.

**Violence that lasts** in the canon tends to be:

- **Specific about mechanism** (Cornwell, Ellroy, Harris) but **selective about duration**. One precise
  detail does more than a paragraph of gore.
- **Costly to the perpetrator and the witness** (Himes, Burke, Paretsky). The body has aftermath.
- **Tied to a system** (Ellroy, Sjöwall & Wahlöö, Larsson). The wound is evidence of a structure.

**Violence that fails** tends to be:

- **Choreographed for admiration** (some Nesbø, some Ellroy imitators). The reader is invited to enjoy the
  technique rather than feel the consequence.
- **Used against characters who exist only to be harmed** ("fridging"). The victim has no life before the
  scene.
- **Detached from procedure** (fantasy violence in a procedural world). The book has taught the reader how
  evidence works, then ignores it.

**Sex** in the canon ranges from absent (Simenon's Maigret, Child) to explicit (Ellroy, Larsson, later
Nesbø). The useful distinction is not "on-page vs off-page" but **whose experience is being rendered**:

- Chandler's eroticism is Marlowe's gaze, and the books are honest that the gaze is a problem.
- Paretsky deliberately refuses to use sex to exploit her characters or readers; V.I. is sexual and moral
  at once.
- Larsson uses sexual violence as an argument about institutional power, but the argument is weakened when
  the camera lingers.
- Ellroy's sex is often grotesque and self-hating, which is consistent with his characters but difficult to
  borrow without borrowing the misogyny.

**The four tests** (expanded in [`04-style-grammar.md`](04-style-grammar.md#9-violence-and-sex-on-the-page)):

1. Whose eyes are we behind, and what would they actually notice?
2. What is the aftermath, and who pays for it?
3. Is the victim a person before and after the scene?
4. Does the scene argue something, or does it merely display?

## 10. Humour and irony

Comedy is not a break from noir; it is one of noir's survival mechanisms.

- **Himes**: black comedy as communal resilience; the joke and the corpse share a paragraph.
- **Leonard**: comedy of self-deception; the funnier the situation, the flatter the prose.
- **McBain**: institutional comedy; head colds, paperwork, squad-room banter.
- **Chandler**: wisecrack as refusal; the joke keeps the narrator from being absorbed.
- **Rankin**: insult as affection; the banter is how people say they care.
- **Ellroy**: grotesque irony; the joke is history's.
- **Mankell**: almost no humour, which makes the rare dry line devastating.

**Rule:** comedy should come from character and situation, not from the narrator's superiority to the
characters. The moment the prose looks down on its people, the reader stops fearing for them.

## 12. The seven failure modes of pastiche

1. **Vocabulary without grammar.** Chandler words in Hammett syntax, or the reverse.
2. **Atmosphere without threat.** Weather, neon, rain, and no one who wants anything.
3. **Cynicism without a code.** Tough talk from a narrator who believes nothing; the reader has no one to
   trust.
4. **Violence without cost.** The body is an event, not a life.
5. **Quirk without psychology.** A detective with a drinking problem, a dead wife, and no specific way of
   thinking.
6. **Mystery without epistemology.** Clues that do not obey the story's own rules of evidence.
7. **Imitation of the surface tics** — the simile, the fragment, the alliteration — without the
   permissions and prohibitions that made them mean something.

The cure for all seven is the same: **write the sentence, then ask what it is doing that a plain sentence
would not do.** If the answer is nothing, cut it.
