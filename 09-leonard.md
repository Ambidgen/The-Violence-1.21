## What the series solved

Leonard solved **dialogue as narrative**. In his best books, the plot advances almost entirely through
people talking, and the narration exists to set up the next exchange. His characters reveal themselves by
what they choose to say and what they avoid, and the reader is trusted to do the rest.

He also solved **the comic crime novel** without turning it into parody. His criminals are vain, inept, and
occasionally competent; his lawmen are tired professionals; the humour comes from the collision of
self-image and reality. Nobody in a Leonard novel thinks they are in a comedy.

His ten rules of writing are famous, and they are mostly a description of his own practice: no weather
openings, no prologues, "said" only, no adverbs on "said," few exclamation marks, no "suddenly," dialect
sparingly, no detailed character descriptions, no long descriptions of places and things, and — the
summary rule — "try to leave out the part that readers tend to skip." His own summary of the method: *if it
sounds like writing, I rewrite it.*

## Syntax and prose mechanics

- **Sentence.** Short and flat, with a conversational rhythm. The prose is deliberately unmemorable so the
  dialogue can be memorable.
- **Paragraph.** Often a single line of action followed by speech. Description is rationed to what a camera
  would need.
- **Dialogue.** The engine and the point. Lots of it, usually tagged "said," often untagged when the voices
  are distinct. Characters talk around the subject; the subtext is the plot.
- **Description.** Minimal. A car, a shirt, a room in one clause. Leonard is not interested in scenery
  unless someone is hiding in it.
- **Figuration.** Almost absent. An occasional comic image, delivered deadpan.
- **Punctuation.** Plain. The comma and the period do most of the work.
- **Exposition.** Hidden in conversation, often as a character explaining a scam to another character who
  is half-listening.

## Character psychology

**Leonard's people** are defined by **competence and self-deception**. The professionals — the marshal, the
bail bondsman, the thief who is good at one thing — are calm, observant, and dry. The amateurs are
convinced they are smarter than they are. The comedy is the gap.

His psychology is anti-romantic. Nobody has a tragic wound; people have habits, appetites, and a story
they tell themselves. The most dangerous character in a Leonard novel is usually the one whose self-story
has no room for other people.

**Antagonists** are often not masterminds but bullies with resources: a drug dealer, a Miami businessman, a
cop who has decided the rules are for other people. Leonard's moral universe is professional rather than
legal: competence is a virtue, cruelty is a vice, and stupidity is a third category that gets people killed.

**Women** in the corpus are frequently sharper than the men and often drive the plot. The series is less
prurient than its contemporaries, but it is still a product of its era.

## Signature devices

1. **Dialogue as action.** Every conversation should change the balance of power or reveal a plan.
2. **The skipped part.** Cut the transition, the explanation, the arrival. Start the scene after the
   decision.
3. **Competence porn.** Show someone doing a job well, in detail, without commentary.
4. **Deadpan comedy.** The funnier the situation, the flatter the prose.
5. **The self-story.** Give every character a sentence they use to justify themselves; let the plot test it.

## What to steal / what to avoid

**Steal:** the discipline of "said"; dialogue that carries exposition; the courage to omit.

**Avoid:** mistaking terseness for style. Leonard's sentences are plain because his ear is doing the work.
Imitators produce dialogue that is merely cryptic and characters who are merely quirky.

## Diagnostic passage (original, in the voice)

> Vince Delano had been stealing cars since he was fourteen and had never once been caught, which he
> attributed to a system. The system was: never work in the rain, never work a street you couldn't
> pronounce, and never let the guy with the clipboard hold the keys.
>
> "You're holding the keys," Vince said.
>
> "I'm keeping them warm," the guy said.
>
> "You're holding them."
>
> The guy looked at the keys in his hand as if he had never seen them before. "So what's the play?"
>
> "The play is you give me the keys, I get in the car, and you go stand somewhere that isn't here."
>
> "And then what?"
>
> "And then I drive away and you tell the police it was a guy with a clipboard."

**Annotation**

- The narration is two sentences of backstory and then gets out of the way. The system is characterisation
  and comedy at once.
- Every line of dialogue either applies pressure or dodges it. The repetition of "You're holding the keys"
  is the whole scene in five words.
- No adverbs on "said"; no description of faces; the only physical action is the look at the keys.
- The last line is a joke that is also a plan. That is the Leonard ending: intelligence disguised as
  casualness.

## Exercise

Write a 400-word scene in which two people discuss a crime without ever naming it. Constraints: at least
70% dialogue; only "said" as a dialogue verb; no adverbs; no description of either character's appearance;
one character is competent and the other is not, and the reader must know which without being told.
